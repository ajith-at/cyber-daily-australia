Hello from Melbourne, Australia. It's Sunday the eighteenth of October, twenty twenty-six, and this briefing covers the twenty-four hours to seven o'clock this morning, Melbourne time.

The lead story is a supply chain compromise that reaches straight into Microsoft 365. ConnectWise has shut off customer access to its Cloud Backup service, formerly known as SkyKick, after an attacker obtained the credential the backup application uses to sign in to customer tenants. Microsoft describes it as an active supply chain attack. The security firm Huntress has confirmed unauthorised sign-ins in at least one hundred and sixty-two tenants, and its latest count, reported by The Hacker News, is one hundred and seventy-three.

The mechanics matter. A backup service needs broad access to do its job: it reads mail, OneDrive and SharePoint files, Teams chats and directory data, and it can write data back during a restore. The attacker didn't need a user's password. They authenticated as the application itself, so multi-factor authentication never came into it, and revoking user sessions does nothing. Huntress says the actor worked through tenants in sorted order, a few seconds apart, on the fifteenth of October, which points to a script running from a target list. It adds that the most interesting victims so far start with the letter A, a rare case where alphabetical order carries a security cost.

In one tenant it got worse. A second ConnectWise application, for managing Microsoft Teams, granted itself Global Administrator and added new secrets to two other trusted applications, one of them an MSP management tool, meaning a tool a managed service provider uses to run many clients at once. That is how one foothold becomes many. ConnectWise says it has seen no sign that stored backup data was compromised.

For defenders, the advice from Huntress, Microsoft and ConnectWise lines up. Disable, but don't delete, the ConnectWise and SkyKick enterprise applications in your tenant. Review service principal sign-ins, which sit on a separate tab from user sign-ins in the Entra admin centre. Then check every application for client secrets or certificates added since the thirteenth of October that nobody on your team created. Disabling the app stops new tokens, but anything already planted stays until you remove it. Australian managed service providers and their clients should treat this as a weekend job, not a Monday one. The application identifiers and the attacker's address are in the show notes.

Second, Drupal. The open-source content management system has patched a critical remote code execution flaw in its core Field UI module, along with a similar flaw in the widely used Entity List module. Drupal core alone isn't exploitable, but contributed modules provide several routes, at least one of which needs no login. The Drupal security team warned in advance that exploits might follow within hours or days. That is relevant here, because Drupal underpins a great many government and university websites, including the Australian Government's GovCMS platform. The fixed core versions are ten point six point nineteen, eleven point three point nineteen and eleven point four point ten, and Entity List three point zero point ten. If you can't update yet, uninstalling the Field UI module is an effective stopgap.

Now to the Australian focus. The Australian Cyber Security Centre published no new alerts or advisories during this window; its most recent alert, on operational technology and edge devices, is from Wednesday the fourteenth. Several incidents do affect Australian organisations and consumers.

Cosmetics brand Napoleon Perdis has told customers that about three hundred and thirty-nine thousand records were exposed after a cyber attack on Retail Express, a third party it formerly used to store customer data. According to the ABC, the attacker had access from November last year to June this year. Exposed details include names, dates of birth, email and postal addresses, phone numbers and purchase histories. Card details and online store passwords were not held on that system, and the Office of the Australian Information Commissioner has been notified. The company has shut the old system down. The practical risk for customers is convincing scam calls and emails using those details. For businesses, it's a reminder that a supplier you stopped using can still hold your customers' data. Other businesses that used the same platform may yet issue similar notices, though that is our assessment rather than anything confirmed.

Next, the Queensland accounting and advisory firm Poole Group says it is responding to a cyber incident after the LockBit ransomware group listed it on its leak site, with a countdown ending on the twenty-fifth of October. The firm has notified the Information Commissioner, the Australian Cyber Security Centre and the Australian Taxation Office. What was taken rests on the attackers' claim alone at this stage.

And a recap of two incidents tied to a newer ransomware group known as The Gentlemen, which Cyber Daily says now ranks second only to Qilin by victim count. The Mandurah State Emergency Service unit in Western Australia has confirmed unauthorised access to a server holding historical records from before twenty twenty-two, and says operations are unaffected. All Souls St Gabriels School in Charters Towers has confirmed an investigation into unauthorised access to part of its systems, with teaching continuing normally.

One update on a story from yesterday. Google's Threat Intelligence Group now attributes the tampering with AOMEI Backupper's update servers to a new cluster it calls U N C seventy seventeen, and lists Australia among eleven countries where organisations were affected. If anyone in your fleet ran that product between April and July, it's worth a closer look.

Turning to global threat activity.

The United States Justice Department has announced its fifth round of action against North Korea's fake remote IT worker scheme. The F B I searched more than forty suspected laptop farms in Texas, seized about one hundred and eighty-seven devices, charged fourteen people and arrested two Americans. One indictment covers more than seventy companies in seventeen states and around three million US dollars in wages sent back to sanctioned North Korean entities. CrowdStrike, which tracks the operators as Famous Chollima, helped with the investigation. The useful detail for hiring managers is that the workers are now recruiting local facilitators and even subcontracting whole jobs, which makes the usual checks less reliable. Watch for reluctance to appear on video, requests to send a laptop to a different address, and pay routed through intermediaries.

On the developer supply chain, the security firm SafeDep has found a new package in a long-running malicious campaign on npm, the JavaScript package registry, called MALFEX. The package, named image decode and written as one word, hides its loader in a compiled Windows add-on rather than in the JavaScript itself, which makes it harder to spot in a code review. Once loaded, it downloads and injects code that leads to an information stealer and remote access tool aimed at browser data, password vault files, cryptocurrency wallets and developer keys. It had been downloaded nine hundred and seventy-nine times and was still available at the time of reporting. The operator's publisher accounts all carry the campaign's own name, which is branding rather than tradecraft. This rests mainly on one firm's analysis, so treat the details as provisional. Anyone who installed it should treat the Windows machine as compromised and rotate credentials.

For e-commerce, the security firm Sansec warns that a maximum severity flaw in Adobe Commerce and Magento, which it calls BlockStrap, could soon be exploited. No attacks have been seen yet. Adobe rates it as a priority two, meaning patch within thirty days, but Sansec found four possible attack paths in under a day and points out that a closely related flaw went from hotfix to mass exploitation in three days. Store operators should apply the October release or Adobe's isolated patch, and keep September's hotfix in place, because the two fixes are separate.

Finally in this section, The Register reports a broader shift towards attacks on physical building systems: heating and cooling, door access and refrigeration. It follows last weekend's attacks on refrigeration controllers at French supermarkets. Figures from the industrial security firm Dragos show ransomware incidents against industrial organisations rising every quarter this year. These figures come from the vendor itself, so read them as a direction rather than a precise count. One incident responder described a ransomware crew that locked a whole office out of its own building after the victim refused to pay. Police eventually got staff back inside by breaking windows, which is one way to restore access.

Now to artificial intelligence security.

A critical flaw has been fixed in Anthropic's Claude Agent S D K for Python, the toolkit developers use to build applications on top of Claude Code. If an application passes an untrusted value as the session identifier when resuming a session, an attacker can slip in an extra command line option that defines a tool server, and that runs arbitrary commands outside the S D K's own permission controls. Version zero point two point one two one fixes it. The general lesson applies well beyond one product: untrusted input should never reach an agent's command line.

In the United States, the National Institute of Standards and Technology, the National Security Agency's Super Intelligence Security Center and CISA, the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency, have jointly published ten voluntary practices for handling what they call control incidents. In plain terms, that's when a highly capable AI agent under training or evaluation takes actions nobody instructed and that undermine the controls around it. The guidance covers limiting an agent's access, monitoring, preserving evidence, rebuilding from known-good baselines, and reporting near misses. Coming in the same week as India's warning about test agents reaching live systems, the direction from governments is consistent: treat agent misbehaviour as an incident, and plan for it in advance.

Attackers are also exploiting interest in AI tools. Research from the analysis platform Any Run, reported by Dark Reading, counts more than twelve hundred cases over six months involving fake AI tools, with Claude impersonated most often, followed by ChatGPT and Codex. Nearly half relied on persuading the victim to paste a command themselves, often a fake installer command aimed at developers. The figures are drawn from the vendor's own platform. The simplest defence is a short, published list of approved AI tools and their official install sources.

And on operational technology, Booz Allen reports that in a laboratory test, commercial AI models worked from the perimeter firewall to moving a robotic arm in sixteen minutes. Separately, Claroty's research team says an AI coding agent chained two known flaws in refrigeration controllers to gain root access in twenty-eight minutes, for about six US dollars in compute costs. These are controlled experiments rather than real attacks, but the point for operators is that obscurity no longer buys much time.

Now to industry and strategic developments. It was a quiet day for deals, with no significant mergers or acquisitions announced in the window. The main vendor stories are the ConnectWise service shutdown and the Drupal and Adobe patches already covered. Administrators rolling out Microsoft's record October updates should also note two known issues: networking problems traced to changes in N T L M authentication, and a break in Windows Smart App Control. Neither is a reason to defer the security fixes, but both are worth testing for.

Finally, the outlook and patch priorities for the next forty-eight hours.

Treat these as critical. First, ConnectWise and SkyKick applications in Microsoft 365: disable them now and review application credentials. Second, Atlassian Data Center products, where the American federal deadline falls today. Third, self-hosted BeyondTrust Remote Support and Fortinet devices, both due tomorrow. And fourth, Drupal core and Entity List.

Rate as high, within seventy-two hours: Adobe Commerce and Magento stores, applications built on the Claude Agent S D K for Python, and any developer workstation that pulled the malicious npm package.

As for what comes next: expect more ConnectWise victims to be disclosed through the week, including clients reached through their managed service providers. Expect working Drupal and Magento exploits to appear soon after technical analysis is published. And in Australia, watch the LockBit deadline for Poole Group on the twenty-fifth.
