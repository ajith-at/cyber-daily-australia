From Melbourne, this is U51, your cyber and AI security brief for Wednesday the 4th of November, covering the 24 hours to a quarter to six this morning, Melbourne time.

We start with a security product turned against its own customers. cPGuard is a malware and firewall tool for Linux web hosting servers, made by a company called OPSSHIELD. On Monday afternoon our time, someone used stolen credentials to get into cPGuard's update system and slipped a malicious script into versions 5.88.12 through 5.88.19. Servers that took one of those updates installed a backdoor and handed over some of their most sensitive files: the password hash file, root's database configuration, root's shell history, and the SSH files that control who can log in as root.

OPSSHIELD says it caught the problem within twelve hours and has cleaned up its own infrastructure. The script sent what it collected to one of OPSSHIELD's own licensing servers, using a compromised account, and the attacker later took at least thirty-two of those files, each tied to a customer server. The company hasn't said how many servers installed the bad versions, and it's still working out how its credentials were stolen.

Version 5.88.25 removes the backdoor. But updating doesn't undo the theft. Any server that may have run the script needs new passwords for root and the database, new SSH keys, and a look at anything sensitive sitting in shell history. The vendor's own advice is to treat a server as potentially compromised even after updating, and notes that a missing or incomplete update log doesn't prove the script never ran.

Australian focus.

Sydney auction house First State Auctions has confirmed that an unauthorised party accessed part of a system holding customer information. That follows a seller on a hacking forum claiming more than a million lines of customer names, emails, phone numbers and addresses last month. The company says the data appears to be mostly basic contact details, there's no evidence card details or passwords were touched, and it has told the Australian Cyber Security Centre and the privacy commissioner. This comes from a single outlet, Cyber Daily, so details may change. If you've bought or bid there, expect phishing that sounds like it knows you.

And a brief Amadeus update. The scale is becoming clearer overseas: Cathay Pacific says around fifty-three thousand bookings were affected by access that ran from August 2023 to September this year, and Japan Airlines puts its figure at around ninety-two thousand passengers. Qantas is still working out whether any of its bookings were involved.

Global threats.

Siemens industrial gateways are under attack. VulnCheck has seen attackers trying full exploits against its decoy devices since the thirtieth of October, aimed at a maximum-severity flaw in SIMATIC IoT2050 Advanced devices running Node-RED, the visual programming tool often used to wire up industrial data. No confirmed victim devices have been reported yet. The Node-RED web interface on affected devices needs no login, so an attacker can create a workflow that runs code with full privileges. Some of the attacks download a Linux bot. Others quietly add a web endpoint that runs any command passed to it, which survives a reboot. Siemens fixed this back in August in version 4.3.4.1. If you can't update, remove Node-RED or lock it down, and keep these devices off the internet. Indicators are in the show notes.

Barracuda has disclosed two flaws that together can give an attacker root on its appliances. The first, rated nine point three, bypasses authorisation on Email Security Gateway. The second, rated eight point six, is a command injection that runs as root, and it affects a wider range: the email gateway, Load Balancer, Message Archiver, Web Application Firewall and its control centre, and Web Security Gateway. The catch for attackers is that they need to reach the admin interface. Barracuda hasn't reported exploitation, but Barracuda's email gateway has been a favourite target before, so patch now and confirm the admin pages aren't facing the internet. Barracuda notes that badly out-of-date devices may need more than one update to catch up.

A follow-up on the Linux kernel flaw we covered last Friday, the race in how the kernel sets up IPv4 TCP connections. Red Hat shipped fixed kernels for Enterprise Linux 8, 9 and 10 on Sunday and now rates the bug critical, at nine point eight, up from moderate. Ubuntu's fixes for its three newest long-term releases are still in an opt-in testing channel, and older Ubuntu releases have none yet. Canonical also says disabling Multipath TCP, which some have suggested, isn't a fix on its own. On Red Hat, update and reboot.

Binance has published its account of how the theft happened, and it's more than one mistake. In June, an engineer searched for a popular AI coding tool and downloaded a trojanised Homebrew installer. Binance caught that and replaced the device, but the attackers had already learned who worked on the wallet team. In September, a second engineer was approached through a real job-hunting platform, sat through two rounds of fake interviews, and was told to download an assignment. The repository ran malicious code during setup, which endpoint protection didn't detect. On the twentieth of October, the attackers used that machine to abuse an internal AI tool and gain control of two colleagues' access, including a container deployment token, and the transfers followed on the twenty-ninth. Binance says it has blocked fifty-seven North Korean attempts against it this year. North Korea denies involvement. For everyone else, a coding test from a recruiter belongs in a disposable machine, never a work laptop.

On the supermarket refrigeration attacks we led with yesterday, the reach now extends to Britain. An industry body for meat suppliers says three members were hit through Copeland XWEB controllers, version 1.12.1 and earlier, for which a firmware fix has been available since August. One British wholesaler says he physically unplugged a fridge while watching it being remotely switched to defrost. Belgium's cyber agency says the attackers may never be identified.

In espionage, ESET describes a Russia-aligned group it calls Gorbag that has focused only on Ukraine since early last year, going after police, justice agencies, front-line local councils and defence with simple data stealers, delivered by phishing about fines and taxes. ESET thinks the group may be harvesting access for others to use later.

And one to watch: customers of the mSecure password manager received an email on Tuesday urging them to consider changing passwords, resetting two-factor authentication and removing passkeys, citing suspicious activity. It doesn't say what happened or whether vault data was involved, and so far there's been no public statement beyond that email. Treat it as a lead only. If you use mSecure, start with your email and banking passwords while you wait for detail.

AI security.

Google's Threat Intelligence Group and Mandiant have put numbers on what they call LLMjacking: attackers stealing access to an organisation's AI model accounts and running up the bill. In some incidents, fraudulent charges passed a hundred thousand US dollars in a single day, and at some organisations they reached a million dollars before access was cut off. Victims span North America, South America and the Asia Pacific, in finance, retail, technology and transport.

The way in is ordinary: exposed or stolen cloud credentials, service account keys, GitHub access tokens, and API keys pushed to public repositories. One group, which Google tracks as UNC6993, got in through a misconfigured service account key, and also ran a fake sign-up page that talked users into pasting a raw access token, which bypasses passwords and multi-factor authentication altogether. Practical steps: put spending alerts and hard quotas on every AI project, scope keys to the models you actually use, and scan your repositories for keys before attackers do.

Next, ransomware with a suspected automated assistant. Bitdefender investigated an attack by a 3AM ransomware affiliate on a school estate in the UK, where about thirty-seven of nearly fifty reachable machines were encrypted in September. Along the way the attacker pulled credentials from three Veeam backup servers, created two backdoor domain administrator accounts, and linked three separate Active Directory domains together to spread further. Bitdefender thinks the attacker was running an automated loop driven by a language model, because slightly altered commands kept arriving every forty to a hundred seconds. It's careful to say that's an inference from behaviour: it couldn't see the attacker's machine, which model was used, or whether a person was simply pasting between the two. The defences are the old ones, starting with protecting backup server credentials.

A brief update on ARTEX, the AI penetration testing framework we covered last Saturday. The Dutch Institute for Vulnerability Disclosure now says ARTEX was the tool behind its own breach in September. It went from first access to root in twelve minutes by chaining two previously unknown flaws, one in the Zammad helpdesk software and one for local privilege escalation, and generated more than four hundred and fifty thousand requests and two and a half thousand scripts. The institute calls it autonomous. To be precise, that's an attack framework running exploit tools in a loop for whoever launched it, with no human approving each step. In the institute's words, it was "super loud and messy", which is good news for anyone watching their logs.

Industry.

Britain's National Cyber Security Centre released its annual report, and its chief executive, Richard Horne, warned of the real prospect of an unprecedented rise in incidents in the coming months. His point is not that AI has invented new attacks, but that it lets old, tolerated weaknesses be found and exploited at scale. The centre judges it highly likely that AI-powered malware will be used in a ransomware attack on UK critical infrastructure by 2028. Horne also noted the Linux kernel project alone patched more than a thousand vulnerabilities last month, which rather supports his case.

And Google has added a top reward of two hundred and fifty thousand US dollars to its cloud bug bounty for flaws that give root access in its cloud.

Outlook and patch priorities.

Critical: cPGuard to 5.88.25, plus fresh passwords and SSH keys on any server that may have run the bad update; and Siemens IoT2050 Advanced to 4.3.4.1, or Node-RED removed.

High: Barracuda patches, with admin interfaces restricted to internal networks; Red Hat kernels for the TCP race; Copeland XWEB firmware; and spending alerts and key scoping on AI projects.

There were no new additions to CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue in this window. The next deadline is the thirteenth of November, for the second Ivanti Connect Secure flaw. Over the next day or two, watch for the Siemens flaw to join that catalogue, a fuller count from OPSSHIELD, Ubuntu's kernel moving to the regular update channel, and more airlines quantifying their Amadeus exposure.
