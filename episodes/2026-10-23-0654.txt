Hello from Melbourne on a Friday, and welcome to U51, the cyber and AI security brief for the twenty-third of October, twenty twenty-six, covering the twenty-four hours to seven o'clock this morning, Melbourne time.

The headlines. PHP to Shell, the pair of critical PHP flaws we led with yesterday, is now being exploited at scale, and Amazon says someone was probing for it four days before the patches existed. Government websites here, in Britain and across Europe went dark as a precaution, including the Australian Signals Directorate's own site. GitLab has fixed a maximum-severity flaw that reaches back twelve years of releases. Microsoft has named a China-aligned group, Storm zero nine four zero, behind the ConnectWise cloud compromise, and says AI agents did much of the legwork. And LiteLLM has explained how its release pipeline was hijacked: through developer tokens that ended up sitting in its own AI gateway logs.

Australian focus first.

On Wednesday night a number of Australian government websites were taken offline while teams patched PHP. Reporting from the ABC, the Canberra Times and Government News names cyber dot gov dot au and the Australian Bureau of Statistics site among them, and New South Wales says some of its sites were also pulled while updates were applied. The ASD said there was no indication those sites had been affected by the vulnerability, and the Industry Minister, Tim Ayres, said plainly that this was not a breach. The ASD's critical alert has been updated to say patches are out, and it tells organisations to find PHP in their environments, apply the fixes and watch for suspicious activity. It also says it is not aware of successful exploitation within Australia. The agency's own advice, to prioritise security over availability, is one it clearly took personally on Wednesday night.

There is one small local footnote in Amazon's research. The earliest exploit attempt it recorded, on the seventeenth of October, came from an internet address geolocated to Australia. That address belongs to a commercial anonymisation network, so it says nothing useful about who was behind it. Treat it as a lead only, and if you hunt, hunt by behaviour rather than by that address.

Separately, Fisher and Paykel Healthcare, the Auckland-headquartered respiratory device maker listed on the A S X, disclosed unauthorised access to two employee email accounts and a subset of company files. It says some files contained personal information and it is notifying the people affected. It found no ransomware or malware, and says manufacturing, supply and patient data systems were not affected.

Now to global threat activity, and the rest of the PHP to Shell story.

The two flaws, CVE twenty twenty-six one zero nine six one eight and one zero nine six one nine, let an attacker with no account run code through the PHP runtime itself, whatever application sits on top. Since disclosure the attack traffic has climbed steeply. Cloudflare's chief executive says the company saw thousands of attempts in the ten hours before the announcement, tens of thousands within forty-five minutes of it, and millions since, with attackers tweaking payloads to slip past web application firewall rules. Security firm Hadrian says the flaws are being exploited at scale, mostly through PHP's session upload-progress feature.

The more uncomfortable detail is the timing. Amazon Threat Intelligence says its honeypot network caught an exploit attempt on the seventeenth of October, four days before the fixes shipped, and saw several different proof-of-concept exploits from around two in the morning UTC on the twenty-first, about twelve hours before release. Varied payloads and infrastructure suggest multiple actors. What nobody has yet published is a confirmed break-in, and CISA had not added the flaws to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue at the time of writing. CrowdStrike warns that a successful attack leaves very little trace, often just one memory-limit error in the web server log. So the practical advice is to review logs and process activity back to at least the seventeenth, not just the day you patched.

The disclosure itself has become a dispute. The PHP Foundation says Chainguard, a supply chain security company that had the fix in advance, published patched builds more than six hours before the agreed release, which let anyone study the fix. Chainguard's chief executive does not dispute the timeline, but says his team independently found the same bugs with a frontier AI model and shipped once exploitation was being reported. A supply chain security firm accused of a supply chain leak is the sort of irony nobody in that room will be enjoying.

The fallout is wide. In Britain, the tech minister told the House of Lords that thirty-two government services were unavailable and four partly so, largely as precaution, with no confirmed compromise. Government sites in Germany, France, Belgium, Finland and the Netherlands also went offline. And appliance vendors are now working through their own products. Palo Alto Networks says all versions of PAN-OS and Panorama are affected, with no fixed releases yet, and the risk is highest where the management interface faces the internet. Fortinet has cleared twelve products, including FortiOS, and is still checking twelve more. Cisco is investigating thirty-one products and cloud services and has not yet confirmed any as vulnerable or safe.

Next, GitLab. On the twenty-first it fixed CVE twenty twenty-six one one zero two six four, rated ten out of ten, which can let an unauthenticated attacker run code on a self-managed GitLab server through an integer overflow in the regular expression engine it ships. It affects every version back to seven point zero from twenty fourteen. GitLab says there is no sign of exploitation, but it has backported fixes to four releases outside its normal support window, which tells you how it rates the risk. One catch: servers patched on the twelfth of October for an earlier, similar flaw need updating again. GitLab dot com is already fixed.

Then a follow-up to ConnectWise, which we covered in Episodes three and four. Microsoft says the intrusion was the work of Storm zero nine four zero, a China-aligned espionage group active since at least twenty twenty-one. After a password spray against Microsoft three six five accounts failed between the tenth and fourteenth of October, the group took over the identities of several ConnectWise cloud applications on the fifteenth and used the access customers had already granted those apps. Using an AI penetration testing framework called ARTEX, it attempted sign-ins to more than two thousand two hundred and fifty tenants, peaking at two thousand nine hundred attempts a minute. In some environments it went from first targeting to privileged cloud access in under three hours, and it changed application credentials in ways that can survive after the original app is cleaned up. Microsoft has not said how many customers were breached or whether data was taken.

A few shorter items. Railway, a platform-as-a-service provider popular with developers, says an intruder may have reached customer environment variables, and in some cases their decrypted contents. It is urging customers to rotate secrets and says the attack looks like part of a campaign aimed at AI inference providers. One researcher reports cryptocurrency wallets with keys stored on Railway were drained; that rests on a single report so far.

On Linux, two remote code execution flaws in KSMBD, the kernel's built-in file-sharing server, were reported on public mailing lists without fixes. One needs no authentication on port four four five. There is no mainline patch or CVE yet, so if you run KSMBD, check whether you really need it. Red Hat Enterprise Linux does not include it by default. The reports were found by an AI scanner and sent straight to public lists, prompting the stable kernel maintainer to reply to two of them seventeen seconds apart, with matching enthusiasm.

Node dot js shipped fixes across its supported lines for ten flaws, led by a bug in the bundled Brotli decompressor the project says could allow code execution, although the public proof of concept only crashed a standard build. The same updates also block the compromised eMudhra keys. Researchers at FoxIO disclosed memory amplification flaws in seven of ten HTTP three stacks they tested, enough to knock servers over with one connection. Quic-go and lsquic have fixes; Bun did not at disclosure.

In the United States, the city of Columbia, the South Carolina state capital, is responding to a cyberattack that took down its citizen services platform, though police, fire, emergency calls and water remain running. And Interpol says Operation Serengeti three, across twenty-one countries, mostly in Africa, led to one thousand four hundred and sixty-two arrests and more than one hundred and five million US dollars recovered.

Over to AI security.

The most useful lesson of the day comes from LiteLLM's account of its compromise. The project says an attacker got into an internal LiteLLM deployment that was exposed to the internet, possibly through a recently patched flaw, and pulled GitHub command line tokens out of its request and response logs. LiteLLM says it observed situations where Claude Code embedded a user's GitHub token in a response body, so logging that traffic captured it. That claim comes from LiteLLM alone, and Anthropic has not commented publicly. With three developers' tokens, including an organisation owner's, the attacker disabled branch protection, ran the release workflow against a lookalike repository and published the poisoned version we reported yesterday. Wiz then saw stolen cloud credentials tested against Amazon Bedrock within hours, which looks like an attempt at free model access. The takeaway: an AI gateway that logs everything is a very complete record of every secret that passes through it. Treat those logs like a credential store.

Second, OpenCode, an open source AI coding agent. Pillar Security found that on Windows and the Windows Subsystem for Linux, a malicious or hijacked M C P server, the Model Context Protocol servers that give agents extra tools, could run commands on a developer's machine during sign-in. It was fixed in version one point one eight point three three in September, but with no CVE or advisory, so scanners won't flag older installs. The vendor says user-configured M C P servers are outside its trust boundary. Developers may feel that boundary sits rather close to their laptop.

Third, the ARTEX detail in the ConnectWise case. Microsoft says it has seen actors from several regions using the same freely available AI testing framework. Nothing about the techniques is new. The speed is.

And a theme worth noting: AI tools found the PHP bugs, a frontier model found them again for Chainguard, and an AI scanner found the KSMBD flaws. Discovery is getting cheaper and faster than the disclosure processes built around it, and this week showed what that looks like.

Industry and strategic developments.

eMudhra, the Indian certificate authority whose intermediate keys turned out to be trivially guessable, has decided to leave the global web certificate business. It says that line brings in about one per cent of revenue, which does make the exit easier. It will replace affected certificates through partners it has not yet named. Chrome will stop trusting the affected root from the morning of the twenty-seventh of October, US Pacific time, and Mozilla made the same call on the twentieth. Around thirty thousand certificates chain to that root, almost all issued through InCommon to universities. If you rely on them, replace them before Chrome's change lands.

In the courts, a New Mexico judge ordered Meta to pay twenty-five billion US dollars over more than forty-three million violations of state law linked to the Cambridge Analytica affair. Meta says it will appeal. And on the vendor side, SentinelOne launched runtime detection and response for AI agents, and Anthropic's Claude Security added continuous scanning for vulnerabilities that reappear in code.

Restrict any Palo Alto management interfaces that face the internet until fixes arrive, and follow the Fortinet and Cisco advisories as they update. Upgrade self-managed GitLab, including servers patched on the twelfth.

High, this week. Replace eMudhra certificates before the twenty-seventh, update Node dot js, rotate Railway secrets, and disable KSMBD where it isn't needed. Review whether your AI gateways log request and response bodies, who can reach those logs, and whether tokens are being redacted. Upgrade OpenCode on Windows and audit its M C P servers.
