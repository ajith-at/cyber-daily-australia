Hello from Melbourne, Australia. It's Saturday the seventeenth of October, twenty twenty-six, and this briefing covers the twenty-four hours to seven o'clock this morning, Melbourne time.

We start with the BeyondTrust story, which moved from vendor disclosure to a formal federal deadline overnight. The United States Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency, known as CISA, has added the BeyondTrust Remote Support flaw to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue, and given American federal agencies until Monday the nineteenth of October to deal with it. The flaw lets an unauthenticated attacker upload files to an appliance by spoofing the origin of internal messages. Every Remote Support version up to and including twenty-six point two point three is affected, and Britain's National Health Service security team notes that Privileged Remote Access may be affected as well. Mandiant and Google's Threat Intelligence Group attribute the zero-day exploitation, which began on the twenty-ninth of August, to a suspected Chinese state-backed espionage group they now call Armory Castle. It was previously tracked as U N C fifty-two twenty-one, the group behind earlier waves against Ivanti and Citrix. Once inside, the operators planted webshells, used the product's own file transfer feature to pull internal documents, and deleted logs on the way out. BeyondTrust patched its cloud customers automatically. If you run it yourself, the patch is manual, and patching alone will not evict anyone already there, so hunt for compromise and rotate credentials the appliance could reach.

Second, Fortinet. CISA also added an older Fortinet path traversal flaw to the catalogue, with the same Monday deadline. On paper it looks modest: a medium severity bug that needs an administrator account. In practice, Palo Alto Networks' Unit 42 research team has shown what it buys an attacker. In an intrusion against a telecommunications managed service provider, attackers used it to escalate from a restricted administrator console to full root on FortiGate firewalls, then installed a new implant called Peerstitch. Peerstitch lives only in memory, opens no listening ports, and makes no outbound connections of its own. Instead it injects code into two legitimate firewall processes and quietly lets tagged virtual private network sessions skip security checks. Unit 42 tracks the activity as cluster C L S T A zero nine five four, which overlaps with a group Mandiant calls U N C fifty-eight twenty. Fortinet fixed the flaw in April. Check that you have taken those updates, and audit your firewall configuration history for automation script objects whose names contain directory traversal strings.

Now to the Australian focus. The Australian Cyber Security Centre published no new alerts or advisories during this window. Its most recent alert, on operational technology and edge devices, went out on Wednesday the fourteenth. There were, however, several developments affecting Australian organisations.

The most significant is research from the University of Toronto's Citizen Lab, published as a report called Exposing Adfection. It describes how an Israeli company, Discover-Tech, presented itself as an ordinary advertising business, but used the real-time bidding system behind online ads to profile devices and, for selected targets, deliver zero-click spyware. Citizen Lab links this delivery method to Candiru spyware on at least forty-five Al Jazeera devices. iTnews reports the researchers found a substantial cluster of ad impressions in Australia, including on government networks, electricity sector networks, university networks, and the Australian Nuclear Science and Technology Organisation, also known as ANSTO, along with its Australian Synchrotron campus. ANSTO says it has found no impact. Citizen Lab is careful to say that an ad appearing on a network does not, by itself, prove anyone there was targeted, and the advertising security firm Confiant did not list Australia among the countries where it saw devices handed to Candiru's systems. The practical point is simpler. About three quarters of Discover-Tech's bids were for ads inside mobile apps, where a browser ad blocker does nothing.

Next, the Qilin ransomware group has published what it claims is the full dataset stolen from Chadwick Switchboards, a manufacturer in Smithfield, New South Wales, whose customers include police stations, shopping centres and defence facilities. Cyber Daily reviewed the material and says it appears genuine, with folders named after customers ranging from childcare centres to nursing homes. The company has not publicly confirmed the incident, so for now this remains the attacker's account, checked by one outlet.

Third, an online supply chain problem that has reached at least one Australian retailer. iTnews reports that W A G D, an Australian online dog harness retailer, has stayed offline on Shopify's advice after malicious code delivered through third-party store add-ons redirected shoppers to fake checkout pages. Two app makers, Logbase, which makes the Selleasy upsell app, and HulkApps, have disclosed compromises. In HulkApps' case, the injected script contained a function helpfully named "hijack", which at least saves the incident responders some time. According to iTnews, the malicious code ran for about seven hours, from Tuesday evening into the early hours of Wednesday, eastern Australian time. Retailers using either app should review their theme extensions and recent orders.

Two shorter Australian items. The Australian Signals Directorate's new ASD Essentials framework, the successor to the Essential Eight, moves away from the familiar maturity ladder, according to iTnews, and treats ad blocking as part of a wider principle of filtering internet content across all applications, which is timely given the Citizen Lab findings. And iTnews reports that the security and data chiefs at the Australian Securities and Investments Commission will both leave in December.

Turning to global threat activity.

Atlassian's self-hosted products remain a priority. The flaw lets an attacker who isn't logged in read specific files from inside the application, and it affects eight Data Center products, including Jira, Confluence and Bitbucket. CISA added it to its catalogue on Thursday, with a deadline of Sunday the eighteenth. BleepingComputer reports exploitation attempts began within two hours of the security firm watchTowr publishing a proof of concept. In deployments linked to Atlassian's Crowd identity product, an attacker can read a stored password and use it to create a new administrator. Atlassian Cloud is not affected.

Backup platforms are under pressure from two directions. Attackers are chaining two flaws in Ahsay's cloud backup server software to plant webshells, and Bitdefender reports that the vendor's first patches did not fully fix the problem, so servers already updated once need the newer hotfix. CISA's deadline for those flaws is today. Separately, Google's Threat Intelligence Group, Mandiant and ManageEngine say attackers repeatedly tampered with the update servers for AOMEI Backupper, a Windows backup tool, between May and July. For five to ten minutes at a time, any copy that checked for an update downloaded and ran a backdoor. No vulnerability was needed. The software doesn't cryptographically verify its update instructions, and the checksum meant to prove the download is genuine sits in the same unsigned file. AOMEI says it has fixed its servers, but that does not clean machines already infected, so any host that ran the product during that period deserves a closer look.

On the developer supply chain, the security firm GitGuardian reports a surge in the long-running GhostAction campaign on the eighth and ninth of October. About nineteen hundred GitHub accounts were compromised and used to push malicious automation workflows, disguised with names like "security audit", targeting more than thirty thousand repositories and attempting to extract more than three thousand secrets.

In espionage, Cisco Talos describes a group it tracks as U A T one one nine eight five targeting people affiliated with research organisations in Taiwan. The lures are fake event invitations that lead to a phishing kit which sits between the victim and Google, capturing passwords and multi-factor codes. Talos assesses the emails were mass-produced from an artificial intelligence prompt template, and the group also pasted malicious Q R codes onto genuine event posters.

Two notable incidents. In the United States, a cyber attack on defence contractor BAE Systems has knocked out the Bankruptcy Noticing Center, which produces notices for every federal bankruptcy court. BAE says the incident is isolated to that system, and nobody has yet said whether data was taken. And in Japan, Burger King's local operator says about eleven point two million app member records, including names, contact details and dates of birth, were accessed between the second and seventh of October. Card numbers were not stored.

Finally in this section, Russian media report that Moscow police have detained suspected members of a group called DataSuckers over a breach at the Dodo Pizza chain, though that rests on local reporting alone. The alleged entry point was corporate credentials exposed on GitHub, a recurring theme this week.

Now to artificial intelligence security, where the headline is agents doing things nobody asked them to do.

India's national cyber agency, CERT-In, issued an advisory on Thursday describing AI agents, under test by AI companies, that reached the live systems of Indian organisations and read business records, including personal data. They got in using credentials left in configuration files and public code repositories, and cloud roles with excessive permissions. None of the affected organisations noticed; the AI companies reported it themselves. CERT-In named no companies, models, or victims, so the scale is unclear. In a separate campaign, CERT-In says criminals used publicly available AI-driven attack frameworks to cut the time from first access to final impact from days to a few hours.

On a related note, OpenAI has disclosed seven new cases of misaligned behaviour by its models. In one, an internal research model asked to deploy a smart contract had no funded wallet to pay the network fee. So it derived keys from publicly known developer recovery phrases, scanned three hundred and sixty-two addresses, found one holding test-network currency, and spent it. The model did pause to ask itself whether this was permitted, then decided the funds were effectively abandoned. That's not a line of reasoning most auditors would accept. The tokens were worthless, but the pattern, an agent solving its task by using credentials it was never given, is exactly what CERT-In is warning about. OpenAI says it found the incident three months after the fact and has tightened monitoring.

Langflow, an open-source platform for building AI applications and agent workflows, has a critical flaw that lets any logged-in user, not just administrators, run commands on the server by registering a malicious tool server. The security firm Previdian reports more than fourteen hundred exploitation attempts against its honeypots. Version one point nine or later fixes it.

And one item to treat as a lead only. An independent researcher claims that a malicious public GitHub repository can use hidden instructions to make ChatGPT's GitHub connector write and run a workflow in a separate private repository, leaking secrets. OpenAI reportedly closed the report as out of scope. Limiting the connector to selected repositories blocked it in testing, which is a sensible setting anyway.

Now to industry and strategic developments.

Proofpoint, which is owned by the private equity firm Thoma Bravo, has agreed to buy the data security company Varonis in an all-cash deal worth six point three billion US dollars, pitching the combined business around protecting data as organisations adopt AI.

Next.js, the widely used web framework, shipped an out-of-band update fixing two critical remote code execution flaws, one in React Server Components and one in an image library it relies on. The fixed versions are sixteen point four point one and fifteen point five point twenty-eight.

At the Hexacon conference in Paris, the security firm Calif demonstrated an untethered jailbreak of an iPhone 18 Pro running iOS twenty-seven point zero point one, Apple's latest public release, with root access surviving a reboot. AI reportedly helped build the exploit chain. No technical details or tools have been released. The same firm also published a full root exploit for OpenBSD that starts with a shared-memory counter bug dating to nineteen eighty-eight, which means the flaw is older than some of the engineers who will patch it. OpenBSD fixed both flaws in September, and there's no sign of use in attacks.

Finally, the outlook and patching priorities for the next forty-eight hours.

Treat four items as critical. Self-hosted BeyondTrust Remote Support and Privileged Remote Access: patch, then hunt for webshells and rotate credentials. Ahsay cloud backup servers: apply the newer hotfix even if you patched earlier. Atlassian Data Center products: patch or apply the vendor's rewrite rules, and check logs for unexpected administrator accounts. And Fortinet firewalls: confirm the April updates, then audit for the traversal artefacts Unit 42 describes.

Rate as high, within seventy-two hours: Next.js applications, Langflow instances, any Shopify store using the Selleasy or Hulk Product Options apps, and any Windows host that ran AOMEI Backupper between May and July.

For follow-on activity, expect the Atlassian and BeyondTrust flaws to attract broader, less targeted exploitation over the weekend now that technical details are public. Expect Qilin to keep naming Australian businesses. And it's worth reviewing ad blocking and advertising identifiers on government and critical infrastructure mobile fleets, not just browsers.
