Same voice, same brief, new name. From today the show is simply U51, which is shorter than most vendor product names and considerably easier to say before breakfast. You'll also find us on X at u51cyber.

The headlines. Canva has confirmed that some enterprise customer records were exposed through a compromised third-party feedback tool. The recruitment software company Bullhorn has taken its platform offline to contain a cyberattack. A trojanised update of a popular WordPress plugin, and fresh detail on the AOMEI backup software compromise, keep supply chain risk at the top of the list. Attackers are exploiting a Plex Media Server flaw chain and a signed Siemens program, and F5 has shipped out-of-band fixes for critical NGINX flaws.

Australian focus first.

Canva, one of Australia's best known technology companies, has disclosed what it calls a third-party data incident. According to Canva, an unauthorised party got into Canny, a tool the company used to collect product feedback, and that tool's connection to Canva's Salesforce account gave access to some enterprise customer records, including business contact details and routine business documentation. Canva says it removed Canny's access, informed affected customers where required, and that its own platform, accounts, passwords and designs were not compromised. A new ransomware group calling itself Seven Deadly Sins listed Canva on its leak site on the sixteenth of October, claiming more than two million Salesforce records, enterprise licence orders and billing documents, and says the data has already gone to a private buyer. Those numbers are the attackers' own and haven't been confirmed. The lesson is a familiar one this year: the weakest point is often a small connected app with a token into the customer relationship system, not the core product. If your organisation is a Canva for Enterprise customer, check for a notification and treat any unexpected invoice or contact request with suspicion.

Next, a follow-up on Saturday's story about spyware delivered through online advertising. The ABC reports that the Australian Nuclear Science and Technology Organisation, or ANSTO, along with its Australian Synchrotron campus, appears among the targets in Citizen Lab's research into Discover-Tech, an Israel-based firm accused of using ordinary ad auctions to profile devices and deliver zero-click spyware from the vendor Candiru. Citizen Lab describes a substantial cluster of ad impressions on Australian government, critical infrastructure, university and media networks, many of them linked to nuclear research. ANSTO says it has found no impact on its systems. The Australian Signals Directorate declined to comment. Note the distinction: an ad impression shows a device was in reach of the targeting system, not that it was infected. For agencies, it's a reasonable prompt to review ad blocking on corporate browsers, which many security teams still treat as a nice-to-have.

Two more items with a local angle. Google and ManageEngine's research on the AOMEI Backupper compromise lists Australia among the countries with affected organisations, in sectors including information technology services, telecommunications and higher education. More on that shortly. And ConnectWise's cloud backup, software as a service security and cloud management products, widely used by Australian managed service providers, remain switched off, with no restoration date yet.

Now to global threat activity.

Start with Bullhorn, a United States company whose cloud platform runs candidate tracking, onboarding and payroll for more than ten thousand staffing firms worldwide. Customers first saw problems with job postings on Friday. By Saturday it was a system-wide outage, and the company has since confirmed a cyber incident and disabled access to contain it, with help from CrowdStrike. Bullhorn says the immediate threat has been eradicated, but it has not yet determined whether customer data was taken. Its core applicant tracking and customer relationship tools, automation and back office services remain down, all non single sign-on users will have to reset passwords, and integration passwords need rotating. Recruitment platforms hold identity documents, payslips and bank details, so this one deserves watching even before any breach is confirmed.

On the software supply chain, two stories. First, Rank Math PRO, the paid edition of a widely used WordPress search optimisation plugin. Copies downloaded or auto-updated during roughly an eleven-hour window on the fifteenth and sixteenth of October carried malware that can rebuild a hidden backdoor every time someone visits the site. The vendor has released a clean version, but installing it does not remove what the malware left behind, including rogue administrator accounts, scheduled tasks, fake core files and even remote login keys outside WordPress. The advice is blunt: if you activated the affected version in that window, treat the site as compromised and restore from a backup taken before the fifteenth.

Second, AOMEI Backupper. Last week Google's Threat Intelligence Group, Mandiant and ManageEngine described how an actor they track as UNC seven zero one seven briefly altered two configuration files on AOMEI's own servers, for five to ten minutes at a time on three occasions between May and July. Any copy that checked in during those windows downloaded a downloader called CROSSROAD and a backdoor called SHINYROAD, and the attackers even updated the expected checksum so the file passed verification. On one occasion they also tried to harvest Windows password hashes. The new detail overnight is AOMEI's response: it now calls the matter high priority, with its chief technology officer leading an investigation, having earlier suggested the files were tampered with in transit. Researchers say the evidence doesn't support that. A server fix stops new infections but does nothing for machines already hit, so check the published indicators if AOMEI software is anywhere in your fleet.

On active exploitation. A chain of four Plex Media Server flaws, nicknamed Plex2Shell, is being used to take over servers without authentication, a day after proof-of-concept code went public. The payloads seen so far are a cryptocurrency miner and a Linux backdoor that doubles as a residential proxy. The fix has been available since the Plex Media Server build released on the third of August, so this is mostly a story about home labs and small offices that never update. Containerised and network storage installs often need a manual update.

Siemens says attackers have abused its Software Center, the tool customers use to install and activate Siemens products. The attackers bundled a genuine, digitally signed copy with their own malicious files, which the program then loaded, and lured victims with ClickFix pages, the fake verification prompts that talk people into pasting commands themselves. Siemens links the activity to a ClickFix delivery platform called Romulus, which Forescout has seen delivering the LegionLoader malware. No Siemens system or update channel was breached, the company says, and version four point zero point nine point eight fixes the weakness. Because the signed file is legitimate, hash blocking won't help; look for the behaviour instead.

F5 has issued out-of-band patches for NGINX, including three critical flaws in the HTTP version three module and the map directive. They affect non-default configurations and mostly cause worker crashes, but code execution is possible where address randomisation is disabled or bypassed. Nothing is known to be exploited yet. There are configuration workarounds if you can't patch straight away, but given how much of the internet sits behind NGINX, this belongs in this week's cycle.

Briefly, a few more. Ahold Delhaize has now confirmed that malicious actors were behind the outages across its grocery brands, has filed a Dutch police report and says it has no evidence that sensitive data was taken; Albert Heijn home deliveries are due back on the twenty-third. Microsoft has confirmed that this month's Windows Server updates are breaking webmail sign-in and mail flow on some heavily loaded Exchange servers. There's a registry workaround, and Microsoft says not to uninstall the updates; one administrator told The Register they spent the entire weekend on their servers, which is not what anyone means by patch weekend. Attackers linked to the GhostAction campaign against GitHub workflows have planted a credential stealer disguised as a font file in ninety-four repositories, set to run when a developer opens the project in their code editor, although one research team thinks this may be a copycat. And a researcher has shown that a flaw in Broadcom's Wi-Fi Protected Setup code lets anyone within radio range run code as root on Broadcom-based home routers. There's no fix or identifier yet, so turning off Wi-Fi Protected Setup is the practical step.

In AI security, three stories.

The case of the former OpenAI engineer accused of planting a recording device in an executive conference room moved on overnight. A San Francisco federal judge ordered Abdulrahman Alfozan held without bail. Prosecutors allege he was in frequent contact with an unnamed member of the Saudi royal family, that a Saudi military official paid for his wife and son to fly home after his arrest, and that government officials met OpenAI executives in that room around the time the device was installed. These are prosecution claims in a detention filing. He is charged with a single count of intercepting oral communications, his lawyers say the travel was booked before any suspicion arose, and he's presumed innocent. OpenAI has said it has no indication he accessed model weights. Whatever the outcome, frontier AI labs are now plainly insider-threat targets, and the physical security of a meeting room counts as much as the data centre.

Elastic Security Labs has exposed a fake Web3 accelerator, tracked as REF eight eight zero one, that targets crypto start-up founders with malicious npm packages offering free Claude and Gemini credits and security audits. The packages install information stealers and backdoors on Windows and macOS, and one repackages a legitimate coding agent so that its model requests run through attacker-controlled services. The audit tool, for good measure, invents security findings. Plenty of human consultants have been accused of the same, but they rarely steal your wallet afterwards.

Check Point Research has disclosed two flaws in LiteLLM, a widely used gateway that sits between applications and model providers and therefore holds a lot of provider keys. One lets a low-privileged user rewrite the proxy's configuration, which can lead to administrative takeover or code execution. The other lets anyone with a valid virtual key redirect outbound requests and leak provider credentials. Both are fixed: version one point eight three point nine covers the pair.

And on frameworks, MITRE has released version twenty of the ATT&CK knowledge base. Prompt injection is now tracked as two techniques, direct and indirect, alongside a new technique for attackers using an organisation's own AI agents to steal data, poisoning web content to sway AI assistants, and coding agent configuration files as a supply chain target. It also adds a campaign covering attacks on water utility controllers and the ransomware group The Gentlemen, which has hit at least two Australian organisations this month.

In industry news, Anthropic has taken on the confidential computing technology of Opaque Systems, which lets AI work on sensitive data under hardware-enforced protection. Reports differ on the structure: some describe an acquisition, while several say it's a licensing deal with key engineers joining Anthropic and Opaque winding down. Terms weren't disclosed.

Now the outlook and patch priorities for the next day or two.

Treat as critical: any WordPress site that activated Rank Math PRO version three point zero point one two three in the window, which means a rebuild rather than an update; Plex Media Server, if it's reachable from the internet; and ConnectWise tenants, where the job remains reviewing application credentials and sign-in logs until ConnectWise publishes restoration steps. Siemens Software Center should update itself on next launch, but confirm it did.

High priority this week: the NGINX patches, LiteLLM, and the Collabora Online built-in server for Nextcloud, which attackers are exploiting to plant web shells. Removing that app is the fix if you can't update; simply disabling it isn't enough. Exchange administrators should apply Microsoft's workaround before the next busy mail day rather than after it.

Things to watch: whether Bullhorn confirms data theft, any regulator notification connected to the Canva incident, AOMEI's promised detailed response, and LockBit's deadline of the twenty-fifth for Queensland's Poole Group. Indicators for today's stories are in the show notes.

That's U51 for Tuesday. Check your plugins, check your backups, and check that your backup software isn't the thing that needs checking. Back tomorrow morning.
