Good morning from Melbourne on Remembrance Day. You're listening to U51, the cyber and AI security brief for Wednesday the 11th of November, covering the 24 hours to about a quarter to six this morning, Melbourne time.

Here's what's making headlines. N-able has taken its hosted N-central platform offline after a researcher published more than thirty unpatched flaws with working exploits. Attackers are using an authentication bypass in Xen Orchestra to take over virtualisation servers. The federal government plans a new regulator to enforce mandatory safety rules on frontier AI companies. Microsoft and Google have each fixed a flaw already being exploited, in Outlook and Chrome. And France has formally blamed Iran for sabotaging supermarket refrigeration at more than 700 sites.

N-able, whose N-central software lets managed service providers monitor and control their clients' computers, took its hosted N-central environments offline on Monday, US time, and told customers running their own N-central servers to take them offline until further notice. The trigger was a public dump on GitHub by the researcher Sean Bales of 33 vulnerabilities he calls Able2Shell, with proof-of-concept exploits. Huntress says they chain into at least four attacks, three ending in remote code execution and one in root access, and the researcher says they can also be used to create unauthorised administrator accounts. N-able had aimed to ship a hotfix around midday on Tuesday, US time, but at its last update it was still validating the fix. It says it has seen scanning tied to the published exploit code but no successful compromise of customers, so check its status page before doing anything. The affected appliance version is in the show notes. Because N-central pushes scripts to every managed client, a compromise here doesn't stay on one server. If you or your provider run it on premises, keep it offline, review logins and new administrator accounts from the 8th of November onwards, and bring it back only when N-able says it's safe. N-able has had a busy few months; this is at least its fourth serious N-central security issue since August.

Vates, the French company behind Xen Orchestra, the management console for XCP-ng virtualisation hosts, says a critical authentication bypass is being actively exploited. Sending a login cookie that contains an empty object makes the server look up the first stored login token and treat the attacker as its owner, with administrator rights. From there, Vates says, an attacker can run code on managed pools and take over XCP-ng hosts as root. The fix is out on both the stable and latest channels, with the exact versions in the show notes. Vates has published host checks and the attacker's infrastructure, also in the show notes. If your console was reachable, assume configuration and credentials were taken and rotate them, and in any case keep Xen Orchestra off the internet.

Australian focus.

The federal government says it will legislate mandatory safety and security obligations for frontier AI companies that want to operate in Australia, enforced by a new targeted AI safety regulator working with the Australian AI Safety Institute. Companies would have to maintain security controls across development and deployment, identify and manage serious risks, notify the government of incidents rapidly, allow independent evaluation of their models, publish safety and security frameworks, and keep up with updated standards. The announcement follows the incident disclosed in September in which an OpenAI agent accessed public and non-public files linked to an older Medicare data portal. The government released the Rapid Review into that incident, with 18 recommendations, and says it accepts all of them, including mandatory reporting of misaligned behaviour and cyber incidents and a look at criminal law reform for serious misconduct. Ministers say the regulator would be able to enforce significant penalties, but no figures or start date have been given yet. Home Affairs has also ordered a government-wide stocktake of systems by March to find configuration weaknesses that AI agents could exploit, which recognises that older systems with weak configuration were part of the problem.

Global threats.

Microsoft's November Patch Tuesday fixes one flaw under active exploitation, a spoofing bug in Outlook. If someone opens a crafted message file, Outlook can connect to an attacker's server without its usual external-content warning, handing over the user's NTLM password hash, which can then be used to impersonate them. The preview pane isn't an attack path; the user has to open the file. Install the November Office updates, and where you can, block outbound SMB traffic and restrict NTLM.

Google has fixed the eighth Chrome zero-day exploited this year, a use-after-free flaw in Chrome's XML handling reported on the 6th of November. Google says an exploit exists in the wild but hasn't said how it's being used. Update Chrome and relaunch it; the fixed build numbers are in the show notes.

SAP's monthly patch day includes four flaws rated the maximum ten out of ten, all exploitable without logging in. Three are in the NetWeaver application server and one in the Web Dispatcher, which takes web requests from the internet. Two can lead to remote code execution, and a flaw in the SAML single sign-on service could let an attacker log in as any user on some systems. SAP shops with internet-facing Web Dispatchers should move these up the queue.

France has attributed the sabotage of refrigeration controllers at more than 700 supermarket sites on the night of the 11th of October to Iran. Its foreign ministry says the attacks were carried out by Shahid Kaveh, an offensive cyber unit of the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps, and that they caused fires and food losses worth tens of millions of euros. France calls it the first offensive cyber campaign carried out purely for sabotage on French soil. It will summon Iran's ambassador and propose further European sanctions, and French agencies have published a joint technical report. Last week we covered the US warning on attacks against industrial refrigeration; this is the first government attribution for the French wave.

ESET has detailed a new toolset it calls Cereal, used by the Russia-aligned espionage group Turla to collect email from Microsoft Exchange servers at government bodies in Kazakhstan and Romania late last year. It runs largely in memory inside legitimate Exchange processes and uses the victim's own server for command and control rather than outside infrastructure, which makes it hard to spot by watching network traffic alone.

Huntress has described attacks on AhsayCBS, a backup server platform sold through service providers, that chained two flaws to bypass login and run commands. The flaws were patched in mid-October but were used as zero-days beforehand. What began as a quick drop of web shells and cryptocurrency miners turned out to be a more patient intrusion, with shells planted in several places, tunnelling tools staged and a custom Python implant built to evade sandboxes, all on servers whose job is to hold backups. Huntress says the intrusions ran in early October, before the hotfixes, and it hasn't identified the implant family. If you run AhsayCBS, confirm you're on the fixed hotfix listed in the show notes and hunt for leftover shells.

Check Point Research has linked three espionage campaigns against telecommunications providers, governments, militaries and research bodies across Latin America, Asia and Europe through a shared malware ecosystem it calls Sparrow. It says a developer it names CopperDragon may be supplying tools to several China-nexus groups, including FamousSparrow, Earth Estries and Tropic Trooper, with activity in 21 countries. That's an analytic link based on shared code and infrastructure, so treat the single-supplier picture as Check Point's assessment for now.

And a mobile item to watch. A researcher has published an iPhone sandbox escape, called worse_query, that lets an app read other apps' files on iOS 26 and 27. The exchange Binance has warned that seed phrases and keys stored by wallet apps could be exposed to a malicious app. The proof of concept is public and the security firm SlowMist warned before Binance did. Apple hasn't said whether this week's updates fix it, and researchers disagree. Nobody reports attacks so far. The escape relies on a shared app container that sideloading tools set up, so remove sideloaded apps and keep everything updated.

AI security.

Fireworks AI, which hosts and fine-tunes models for customers, has widened its account of an October breach. It first reported that attackers had captured stored secrets from evaluation jobs. Now it says the attackers also reached account and billing information, and, for a limited set of customers, secrets, datasets and incomplete model weights. A second wave on the 24th of October came through a third-party cloud provider hosting its research machines. Fireworks links the activity to a financially motivated group that resells stolen access to large language models, a practice known as LLMjacking. Payment card numbers weren't stored, and the company says its inference service, training software kit and Nexus platform weren't affected. It has also switched off self-serve dataset uploads and downloads for batch and dataset-based training. If you've used Fireworks for batch or dataset-based training, rotate any secrets you gave it and watch for your notification.

On the defensive side, Google DeepMind says AI agents have formally verified key security properties of the actual code behind the Signal protocol, which protects messages in Signal and Google Messages. Formal verification means mathematically proving that code does what its specification says. DeepMind's framework, AlphaProof Code, had human experts write and review the security goals, while agents produced over six million lines of machine-checked proof and flagged about one percent for human review. The work took four weeks, and DeepMind says it is the first time a widely deployed protocol's implementation, rather than just its design, has been verified this way. As a test, the team put two previously fixed real bugs back into an isolated copy of the code, and in both cases the system proved the buggy version insecure. Signal's chief technology officer welcomed the result. DeepMind is open-sourcing the proofs. A proof covers only the properties that were specified, so it raises assurance rather than guaranteeing there are no bugs.

Industry and research.

Researchers found administrator credentials for a ForgeRock identity server inside a carmaker's Android app, and used them to take over customer accounts and unlock and start connected cars, starting from nothing more than a number plate. The credentials were meant for a staging system but worked against production. The researchers, one of them based in Sydney, say the carmaker's Australian site was easier to exploit than its American one because of a weaker web application firewall. The carmaker, which sells two brands in Australia and hasn't been named, fixed the flaw within hours of the report.

Outlook and patch priorities.

Critical today: keep on-premises N-central servers offline until N-able clears them, and patch Xen Orchestra and run the host checks. Then the Outlook and Chrome fixes, and SAP's ten-out-of-ten flaws for anyone with an internet-facing Web Dispatcher. Expect scanning against N-central now the exploit code is public, and watch for N-able's hotfix and more fallout from the France attribution, including possible sanctions.
