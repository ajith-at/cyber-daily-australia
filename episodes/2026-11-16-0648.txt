Good morning from Melbourne. This is U51, your cyber and AI security brief for Monday the 16th of November, covering the weekend and the 24 hours to about seven this morning, Melbourne time.

Here are the headlines. Cursor says the poisoned Claude Code extension in its marketplace traces back to an access key stolen in the Fireworks AI breach. Palo Alto Networks says Chinese-speaking attackers let an open-source AI framework run intrusions largely on its own, getting in at ten organisations. Airtasker says customer support records, including some identity documents, were taken from a third-party system. And Amazon Web Services had three hours of sign-in and connectivity trouble in its Northern Virginia region overnight.

First, the weekend in brief. Saturday's Episode 30 led with the Check Point gateway zero-day chained with the earlier PHP2Shell flaws to plant backdoors and, for some victims, wipe VMware hosts. It also covered the US disruption of North Korea's FaceStealer deepfake video-call scheme and the move to seize more than 1.2 billion US dollars stolen from Binance, PostgreSQL's 52 security fixes, and the cyberattack on German drone maker Quantum Systems. Separately, US officials said Friday's nationwide air traffic ground stop was caused by a communications problem, with no sign of a cyberattack, though that rests on press reports of officials' comments.

Sunday's Episode 31 was a Sunday Special, Open-Weight AI Models, Use and Risk, which opened with the end of the Microsoft 365 outage and the phishing that followed it. Its five takeaways: one, self-hosting moves the black box inside your perimeter but does not make it trustworthy, so you inherit the provider's security job. Two, know exactly what you run, from approved sources with verified provenance, safe file formats, and a register with an accountable owner. Three, test the specific version for the job, and treat fine-tuned, quantised, merged or abliterated copies as different models. Four, put the controls outside the model: deny outbound traffic by default, isolate it, give it least-privilege tools require human approval for irreversible actions, and enforce on the action, not the reasoning trace. And five, monitor, rehearse your response, and be willing to say no when the remaining risk can't be brought within appetite. The full episode and notes are on u51 dot org.

Now to this morning's news. Cursor has explained how a trojanised build of Anthropic's Claude Code extension, which we reported at the end of October, ended up in its marketplace. The attacker obtained an access key Cursor had issued to Fireworks AI, the model hosting company breached in recent months, and used it to copy a storage bucket of coding agent traces from sessions that were not in Cursor's Privacy Mode, most dating from 2025. Some of those traces still contained credentials, exposing the keys of fewer than one in ten thousand customers, and the attacker used them to reach some of Cursor's software-as-a-service tools, archived non-production code, support emails and credentials customers had sent to support. From there it planted the malicious extension on the cache serving the marketplace for about eleven hours on the 29th of October, potentially reaching at most five thousand users. Cursor has moved its inference traffic off Fireworks, cut off the vendor's access and says it has seen no malicious activity since the 1st of November. Uber has confirmed data theft through a service provider that TechCrunch reports was Fireworks, and Notion has confirmed its data was stolen in the Fireworks hack. Agent session logs collect whatever secrets pass through them, and anything shared with a model provider is only as safe as that provider's security.

Australian focus.

Airtasker says an unauthorised party accessed the third-party system it uses for customer support enquiries and probably downloaded customer information. It found and closed the access on the 10th of November. For most affected people the data is contact details and the content of support tickets, but in a small number of cases it includes identity documents, card numbers or passwords that customers had typed into support messages themselves. Card details stored in Airtasker accounts were not involved. The company says the intruder appeared to be preparing a payment scam, including fake Airtasker emails asking for bank details, so treat any message about a payment on hold with suspicion. Airtasker is emailing those affected; if you once sent a password to its support team, change it there and anywhere else you reused it.

Expedia Group, which owns Wotif, has confirmed that attackers reached its systems through a compromised third-party data platform provider. An extortion group calling itself n0n claims to hold hundreds of millions of loyalty and account records, and Cyber Daily reports Australian email addresses in material it has posted. None of the figures is verified, Expedia has not said whether its incident is the one the group describes, and rows of data are not the same as people.

Global threats.

Amazon Web Services reported sign-up errors, authentication failures and connectivity problems in its US-EAST-1 region, in Northern Virginia, from about ten past ten last night until just after one this morning, Melbourne time. Customers who sign in through IAM Identity Center, Amazon's single sign-on service, could not reach the console or services that rely on it, such as Kiro and Amazon Connect, and a subset of network load balancers in one availability zone stayed affected for longer. Amazon's health dashboard now shows the incident as resolved, and it has not yet given a root cause.

Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company, the world's largest contract chipmaker, has confirmed that data on current and former employees was published online without authorisation. The company says the data matched information held by a cloud service provider it uses and that it has found no evidence of a compromise of its own systems or any effect on operations. A seller on a hacker forum claims 46,000 employee records, including national identity numbers and banking details, and set a public leak deadline of the 15th of November; that figure is the seller's own.

UnrealIRCd, the open-source chat server, is the other flaw on today's US federal deadline. Any user on a network with two or more linked servers can inject commands that the servers then carry out with full server authority. The maintainer released the fix on the 7th of November, saw attackers exploiting it the next day, and calls it the most critical issue in the project in fifteen years. The fixed version and the interim spam filter workaround are in the show notes.

Shipping giant Maersk says data was taken from a local legacy system that came with an earlier acquisition, and that operations are unaffected. The WhiteArmor extortion group claims about 100 gigabytes from the platform of LF Logistics, which Maersk bought in 2022, and is demanding five million dollars. Maersk has not confirmed the group's claims.

AI security.

Palo Alto Networks' Unit 42 says Chinese-speaking attackers used Cairn, an open-source AI penetration testing framework, to run 25 operations against at least 19 organisations in August and September. The operators gave each task a target and a one-line goal, and Cairn's agents, driven by Zhipu's GLM and DeepSeek models, planned the steps, ran the tools and recorded what they found, with humans adding hints when needed. At ten organisations the attackers got in or took data, including valid credentials, more than a million personal records at one victim, new administrator accounts in a production system and an edit to a live industry standards ballot. Targets included semiconductor supply chain firms, US defence policy groups and civil society. Unit 42 assesses, with moderate confidence, that the activity is China-nexus intelligence collection. As with the Artex framework the FBI flagged on Friday, the capability comes from the agent software and the tools and access it is given. Identity controls, egress limits and monitoring of unusual automated activity are what blunt it.

Industry and patches.

Microsoft begins removing the remaining eMudhra root certificates from Windows, Edge and Azure today, following the certificate authority's key compromise. Systems that don't receive automatic trust list updates need a manual update, and anything still chained to those roots will stop being trusted by default.

Outlook and patch priorities.

Two US federal deadlines fall today: first, Check Point gateways: apply the fixed Jumbo Hotfix, and treat any gateway left unpatched since late October as possibly compromised, checking connected VMware hosts as well; second, UnrealIRCd, on any network of linked servers. Beyond those, if you used Cursor's marketplace on the 29th of October or have shared credentials with Fireworks AI, follow the vendors' notices and rotate. AWS customers who rely on Identity Center should review whether break-glass access to their accounts worked overnight. And Airtasker users should expect convincing payment scams.
