Hello from Melbourne, Australia, where the Bureau of Meteorology promises a sunny twenty-eight degrees and a stiff northerly, so hold on to your hat on the way to work. It's Monday the nineteenth of October, twenty twenty-six, and this briefing covers the twenty-four hours to seven o'clock this morning, Melbourne time.

First, a quick catch-up for anyone who took the weekend off. Saturday's edition led with the BeyondTrust Remote Support zero-day, which the United States Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency, or CISA, added to its catalogue of known exploited vulnerabilities, and which Mandiant links to a suspected Chinese espionage group. It also covered Fortinet firewalls compromised with a memory-only implant, and Citizen Lab's research on spyware delivered through online advertising. Sunday's edition led with the theft of a ConnectWise Cloud Backup credential that let an attacker sign in to Microsoft 365 tenants, alongside critical Drupal patches, the Napoleon Perdis customer data exposure and a LockBit claim against Queensland's Poole Group. The weekend itself was quiet for new incidents, but the ConnectWise story grew, so that's where we start.

ConnectWise Cloud Backup, formerly known as SkyKick, is used mainly by managed service providers to back up their clients' email, files and Teams data. On Friday it emerged that an attacker held the credential the backup application uses to sign in to customer tenants. Over the weekend the picture got bigger. The security firm Huntress now says almost one thousand Microsoft Entra tenants show sign-in activity from the attacker's address, and more than three hundred of those sign-ins succeeded. That's up from the one hundred and sixty-two confirmed tenants reported on Friday.

Two new details stand out. First, Huntress describes a second, targeted wave on the fifteenth of October against sixteen tenants in the policy, national security, trade and critical minerals sectors. Those attempts failed, mostly because the organisations didn't use ConnectWise, so the application wasn't there to abuse. Second, the same address was used for password spraying against four law firms on the thirteenth and fourteenth. A list that runs from managed service providers to critical minerals looks less like opportunistic crime and more like an actor with collection interests, but nobody has attributed the activity, and that reading is our assessment rather than anything the investigators have said.

ConnectWise says it disabled seven of its application identities on Saturday evening, Australian time, covering Cloud Backup and its Cloud Management apps for Exchange Online, Teams, SharePoint, Microsoft Graph and Partner Center. It has also taken its SaaS Security and Cloud Management services offline. Backups aren't running and restores aren't available, which is an awkward position for a backup product. The company stresses that stored archives remain encrypted and show no sign of access, but it also says, fairly, that it cannot see inside customer tenants. Its latest update adds further attacker addresses from partner monitoring, and the next is due by noon today, Melbourne time.

The practical point hasn't changed, and it matters more now. Disabling the vendor's apps stops new sign-ins, but tokens already issued can keep working until they expire, and anything the attacker planted stays put. In the one tenant Huntress examined closely, new client secrets were added to two other trusted applications, set to expire in twenty twenty-eight. So, check service principal sign-ins from the fifteenth of October onwards, and look for any application credential added since the thirteenth that nobody on your team created. The addresses and application identifiers are in the show notes.

Now to the Australian focus. iTnews notes that Huntress raised the alarm over the weekend, Australian time, and the ConnectWise toolset is common among local managed service providers, so expect Australian clients to be part of the clean-up even though none has been publicly named. The critical minerals targeting deserves attention here too, given how central the sector is to Australia's economy and to its partnerships with the United States. No Australian organisation has been reported among the sixteen targets.

Separately, and catching up on a story from late last week, Hunter Neurology, a neurodiagnostic practice with clinics in East Maitland, Shortland and Port Macquarie, is investigating a claim by a new ransomware group called n0n, spelled with a zero. According to Cyber Daily, the group says it holds more than fifteen thousand patient images and scans, almost twelve thousand letters and reports, and copies of the practice's medical records backups. The practice says the claim appears to relate to a third-party provider that hosts its systems, that patient services are running normally, and that it will contact anyone affected. The group has shown no evidence so far, so the volumes are its own claim. n0n emerged only in late September and has listed more than eighty victims since, which makes it one of the busiest newcomers around. On Sunday it posted seven of the fifteen leak-site listings recorded by one tracker. It also publishes firm instructions on how its name must be capitalised, which shows a care for detail it doesn't extend to other people's data.

Turning to global threat activity.

Attackers are exploiting a critical command injection flaw in SiteGuard Server Edition, a host-based web application firewall from a leading Japanese vendor. The bug sits in the statistics feature, which is on by default, and gives an attacker commands with root or system privileges. Both the vendor and Japan's national computer emergency response team, JPCERT, confirm exploitation, though neither has said who is behind it or how widely. Fixed builds are eight point zero zero Update five, or seven point zero zero Update six on FreeBSD, and there's a file-replacement patch for older and end-of-life versions. One useful instruction from the vendor: download the product's diagnostic information before you patch, because the fix can erase traces of an attack. A security product that becomes the way in isn't a new story, but it remains an uncomfortable one.

On the retail front, Ahold Delhaize is recovering from the incident that took its systems offline from the eleventh of October. NL Times reports that the Albert Heijn website and app came back on Sunday, with online deliveries due to restart on Friday. In the United States, local media report that some of its grocery banners still have websites and apps offline. The company says it has no indication that customer or employee data was involved.

In Canada, the western grocery chain Save-On-Foods has told members of its More Rewards loyalty program that names, and where provided, addresses, phone numbers, email addresses and dates of birth were accessed after unauthorised activity on one of its servers on the thirteenth of October. Card details and passwords were not affected, according to the company, which hasn't said how many people are involved.

And in healthcare, a Texas Attorney General breach filing for Oracle Health's legacy Cerner systems lists almost three million Texans affected, and reporting at the weekend puts the nationwide total at about nineteen point nine million people. Oracle has not confirmed that national figure publicly, so treat it as provisional. The intrusion dates to early twenty twenty-five and involved stolen customer credentials used against a server that hadn't yet been migrated to Oracle's cloud.

Now to artificial intelligence security, where the theme this weekend is that A I keys have become a currency.

OpenCode, a popular open-source A I coding agent, says a breach late on Friday, United States time, exposed provider keys saved by about one point four per cent of users of its paid Console service. These are the users' own keys for OpenAI, Anthropic, Google and other model providers. OpenCode says an attacker reached a legacy system it was in the process of retiring, and it has linked the incident to what it calls a large-scale distillation attack, a term for harvesting a model's answers to train a rival model, and one that used to belong to whisky makers. Before the company said anything publicly, some users reported unexplained charges on their accounts, ranging from a couple of hundred to more than a thousand US dollars. Those individual figures haven't been independently verified. OpenCode says it is working with the three main providers on remediation and has emailed everyone affected.

If your developers use OpenCode Console, revoke and reissue every provider key they linked, in each provider's own dashboard rather than through a link in an email, and set hard spending limits. It fits a pattern from last week, when Symantec reported that the Aoki information stealer now collects credential files for A I coding assistants, including Claude, Codex, Cursor and Gemini. Stolen A I keys can be cashed in directly, and they can also lead to the code repositories and cloud services connected to them.

On the research side, a new academic paper examines tool servers built on the Model Context Protocol, the standard that lets A I agents call external tools. Of almost forty-eight hundred servers they triaged, the authors flagged seven hundred and eighty-seven that reached the host's file system despite not presenting themselves as file tools, and they confirmed one hundred and fifty cases where crafted inputs, such as a prompt injection, made a harmless-looking tool return files from outside its intended scope, including planted credentials. Two of the reported findings became vulnerabilities in Anthropic's Git server for the protocol. This is a preprint that hasn't yet been peer reviewed, but the lesson is a familiar one: approving a tool by its description doesn't limit what the server process can read, so run tool servers sandboxed and with the least file access they need.

Now to industry and strategic developments.

SecurityWeek reports figures from the recruitment firm CyberSN showing United States cybersecurity job postings fell twenty-six per cent in the first nine months of the year, to a four-year low. Demand for A I security engineers rose by more than two hundred and sixty per cent over the same period. The report counts postings rather than explaining them, but the direction is clear enough.

And one item to treat as a lead only. The forensics company Cellebrite reportedly presented a boot ROM exploit at the Hexacon conference in Paris, affecting Apple chips from the second-generation iPod touch through to the iPhone eleven. Because the flaw sits in read-only memory, it can't be patched, but it needs physical access, so the exposure is to lost, stolen or seized devices. There's no technical write-up yet, and the claim rests on conference coverage alone.

Treat these as critical. SiteGuard Server Edition: save the diagnostic information, then patch. And self-hosted BeyondTrust Remote Support and the affected Fortinet products, where the United States federal deadline falls today.

Rate as high, within seventy-two hours: any OpenCode Console provider keys, which should be rotated now, Drupal core and the Entity List module, and Adobe Commerce and Magento stores.

As for what comes next: expect more ConnectWise victims and more indicators to surface through the week, including clients reached through their managed service providers, and watch for any attribution given the sectors involved. Expect n0n to keep naming and publishing victims at its current pace. Expect OpenCode to say more about how the keys were stored, and expect stolen A I keys to be used quickly. And in Australia, LockBit's deadline for Poole Group falls on Sunday the twenty-fifth.
