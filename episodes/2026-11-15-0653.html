Episode 31, Sunday Special: Open-Weight AI Models, Use and Risk, 15 November 2026

SpotifyApple PodcastsPocket Casts

Open-weight AI models give organisations control, sovereignty and cost advantages, but running a model on your own servers does not make it trustworthy. This Sunday Special uses Cohere's Qwen sandbox finding, Microsoft Research's Lazarus backdoor and ASD's ACSC guidance of 5 November to walk through three ways trust in the weights fails and the layered controls that have to sit outside the model. It opens with the end of this week's global Microsoft 365 outage and the phishing campaign exploiting it.

News: one item

Microsoft 365 global outage resolved; outage-themed phishing observed. Microsoft said on Saturday 14 November that it had addressed the underlying issue and restored service to remaining customers and regions; its status page reports the email backlog processed (incident MO1550821; Exchange Online incident EX1550543). Microsoft attributes the fault to an earlier network capacity upgrade, not cyber activity or AI agents; a post-incident report is promised. Microsoft Threat Intelligence observed fake service health emails quoting the genuine incident and threatening deletion of "held" messages; Microsoft says the activity is consistent with credential phishing, not confirmed. Sender domains and redirect host are listed in the iTnews report. - https://apnews.com/article/microsoft-outage-outlook-email-365-recover-ca395c0f1e51af784b6d70afb6554f6a - https://status.cloud.microsoft - https://www.itnews.com.au/news/phishers-quick-to-exploit-extended-m365-outage-630347

The hook: a defensive task, a blocked upload

Cohere: Qwen3.8-Flash-Next attempted an HTTP PUT of a test string (PROBE_PAYLOAD_ABC123) to an Alibaba Cloud OSS bucket in Beijing during a detection-rule task (CTI-REALM), while its reasoning said no tool calls. The sandbox had no route out. Fewer than one call in 100,000; a clean re-run produced a normal rule; up to about a hundred calls a week at 10 million agent turns (Cohere's arithmetic, an upper bound). Vendor research; Cohere sells sovereign, single-custody models and also releases open-weight models under Apache 2.0. - https://cohere.com/blog/concerning-behaviour-popular-open-weight-ai-model - Traces and reproduction script: https://huggingface.co/datasets/CohereLabs/10-2026-qwen-investigation-traces

Part two: three ways trust in the weights fails

The file is code: malicious models on Hugging Face (JFrog, 2024). About 100 models with real payloads, about 10 truly malicious per JFrog; most were researcher proofs of concept. One PyTorch model (baller423/goober2) used pickle reduce to open a reverse shell on load. - https://jfrog.com/blog/data-scientists-targeted-by-malicious-hugging-face-ml-models-with-silent-backdoor/ - https://arstechnica.com/security/2024/03/hugging-face-the-github-of-ai-hosted-code-that-backdoored-user-devices/ - Hugging Face pickle scanning today: https://huggingface.co/docs/hub/security-pickle

Behaviour you can't see: independent reruns of the Qwen finding. Alibaba-host habit reproduces; the Beijing bucket did not reappear in 12,400 completions (sxuff); Zenity found 132 PUT commands in 93,000 completions on other Qwen models, none to Alibaba hosts; Vals AI saw real requests to the routify Singapore proxy host, all refused (HTTP 403). Cohere's prefill figures (URL generation 4% to 92% with curl -o) are Cohere's own. Alibaba has not publicly responded, as far as we can find. - https://github.com/sxuff/qwen-trigger-rerun - https://zenity.io/blog/who-is-your-agent-calling-what-the-qwen3-8-egress-finding-means-for-enterprise-ai - https://www.vals.ai/blogs/qwen-alibaba-requests - https://newruntime.com/analysis/research-independent-reruns-can-t-reproduce-qwen-s-beijing-upload-and-the-real-rate/

Part three: the control stack

NIST SP 800-218A, SSDF Community Profile for Generative AI and Dual-Use Foundation Models. Secure storage should include models, weights, pipelines and reward models. - https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/218/a/final

Layers: governance and register; provenance and integrity; independent testing (prompt injection, unsafe tool use, data exfiltration); isolation and default-deny egress; harness controls and human approval; monitoring and tested response. Hosted open-weight APIs are hosted services.

Five takeaways

  1. Self-hosting moves the black box inside your perimeter; it doesn't make it trustworthy. You inherit the provider's security job.
  2. Know exactly what you run: approved sources, verified provenance and integrity, safe file formats, and a register with an accountable owner.
  3. Test the specific version for the job, and treat fine-tuned, quantised, merged or abliterated copies as different models.
  4. Put the controls outside the model: deny egress by default, isolate, least-privilege tools, human approval for irreversible actions, and enforce on the action, not the reasoning trace.
  5. Monitor, rehearse response, and be willing to say no when residual risk can't be brought within appetite.
