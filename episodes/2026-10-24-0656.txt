Good morning from a showery Melbourne. This is U51, your cyber and AI security brief for Saturday the twenty-fourth of October, twenty twenty-six, covering the twenty-four hours to seven this morning, Melbourne time.

The headlines. Fortinet has shipped critical fixes across its firewall and management range for flaws found with frontier AI models, and it is holding back the details until November so attackers can't simply read the patches. The Australian Signals Directorate has rated it a critical alert. PHP to Shell is still drawing millions of attempts, but no confirmed successful break-in yet, and PHP has flagged one more, narrower problem with no patch. CISA has added two actively exploited flaws to its catalogue, in Arista's VeloCloud Orchestrator and in a Collabora add-on for Nextcloud. Researchers have exposed a ransomware affiliate running its intrusions through AI agents, with a human clicking approve. And Proofpoint is buying Varonis for six point three billion US dollars.

Australian focus first.

On Friday the Australian Signals Directorate's Australian Cyber Security Centre, the A C S C, issued a critical alert covering Fortinet products. Fortinet has released what it calls a security hardening release for FortiOS, FortiManager, FortiAnalyzer, FortiMail and FortiAuthenticator. The fixes include critical and high-severity flaws that Fortinet found using frontier AI-assisted testing, and it thanks Anthropic, OpenAI, Google and SpaceX for working with it. What is unusual is the sequencing. Fortinet is publishing the fixed versions now, but the vulnerability details only on the tenth of November, on the argument that AI tools can now diff a patch and produce a working exploit in hours or days. So for the next two and a half weeks, defenders know there is something serious in there, and so does everyone else, but nobody outside Fortinet is meant to know exactly what.

For FortiOS, the fixed versions are eight point zero point two, seven point six point eight, seven point four point thirteen and seven point two point fourteen, with matching releases for the other products listed in the show notes. FortiSASE has been updated automatically, but FortiManager may need a manual update. The A C S C says it is not aware of exploitation in Australia. If you can't patch straight away, restrict the management interfaces to trusted addresses and make sure FortiManager is not reachable from the internet. And given how often Fortinet edge devices have featured on this show, the safe assumption is that someone is already diffing these builds.

Second, the government has sanctioned two North Korean individuals, Yu Pu Ung and Sim Hyon Sop, and two companies that place North Korean IT workers in remote jobs abroad to earn foreign currency. iTnews reports the Australian Sanctions Office has warned that these workers are using stolen and doctored Australian driver licences and passports to win remote contracts. Making assets available to a listed person or entity can carry up to ten years in jail. For anyone hiring remote developers, this is a nudge to check identity documents properly, and to be wary of the candidate whose camera is always broken.

Third, a follow-up on the advertising-based spyware campaign we covered back in Episode 2. The ABC says a small number of its staff devices were shown ads from the network that Citizen Lab links to the Israeli company Discover-Tech, in the campaign it calls Adfection. The ABC says it has found no sign of compromise and is wiping the devices that are still in service as a precaution. ABC reporting puts more than two dozen Australian government, critical infrastructure, university and media organisations in the targeted cluster, though those others have not been publicly confirmed.

And a reminder on PHP. The Essential Eight expects critical flaws in internet-facing services to be patched within forty-eight hours of a fix. For the PHP releases that landed at one in the morning on Thursday, that window closed at one o'clock this morning. One brighter local note: AGL says half a million customers have now enrolled passkeys, about thirty per cent of its users, and that they log in around thirty seconds faster.

Now to global threats, starting with PHP to Shell, the pair of critical PHP flaws we have led with for the past two days.

The picture has become more nuanced. Cloudflare, Amazon and others still report millions of exploit attempts. Arctic Wolf ties many of them to a botnet called Potassium, and others to a Mirai variant, both trying to recruit web servers into denial-of-service networks. But experts speaking to The Record say none of the attempts they have seen succeeded, and that most public proof-of-concept code only works in lab conditions. The main obstacle is address space layout randomisation, a memory protection that makes this kind of bug much harder to turn into reliable code execution. Google's threat intelligence team says it has not validated exploitation in the wild, but warns that limited exploitation may already have begun. And VulnCheck says it has developed its own technique to bypass that protection, which shows the barrier can be overcome. The message is the same as yesterday: a hard exploit is not an impossible one, so patch.

PHP has also disclosed a separate issue, tracked as CVE twenty twenty-six, one one five five six zero. It only affects applications that change their session serialisation handler at runtime in a particular way, and PHP thinks that setup is unlikely. There is no emergency patch. The fix is to turn off session upload progress, the same mitigation PHP already recommended on Wednesday, so if you applied it then, you are covered.

Next, two additions to CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue on Friday, both with a due date of Monday.

The first is in Arista's VeloCloud Orchestrator, the management system for VeloCloud SD-WAN networks. It is a SQL injection flaw rated nine point nine that lets anyone with a login, even a read-only customer account, gain operator privileges, the top level of control. It is the third exploited zero-day in the orchestrator since July, and the fixes for the earlier two do not cover it. Hosted orchestrators are already patched. On-premises customers need the new releases listed in the show notes.

The second is in the Collabora Online built-in CODE server, an optional Nextcloud app that runs a document server on the Nextcloud box itself. Attackers have been planting web shells on Nextcloud servers since September, using a command injection that works only while the local document server is down, for example during a restart or upgrade. Collabora says disabling the app is not enough. Update it to version twenty-six point four point three zero three, or remove it.

Hewlett Packard Enterprise has patched seventy-two flaws in ArubaOS, the software behind Aruba wireless controllers, gateways and access points, including eight rated maximum severity that allow unauthenticated code execution or authentication bypass. There is no public exploit yet. HPE wants management interfaces confined to a dedicated network segment, which is good advice for every device in today's episode.

On the edge-device theme, Group-IB and NyxLab have described a campaign by a criminal actor known as k0t, who has been enumerating exposed Fortinet and Palo Alto Networks firewalls and VPNs at scale. Reporting puts the exposed credentials at more than seventy thousand, with about thirty-eight per cent of victims in the Asia-Pacific region. Many victims later appeared on the leak sites of the Qilin and The Gentlemen ransomware groups, which suggests k0t sells or uses that access, though that link is inferred rather than proven.

A follow-up on Friday's GitLab story. Ruby has released versions four point zero point nine, three point four point twelve and three point three point thirteen, fixing three bugs in Onigmo, its built-in regular expression engine, which sat behind GitLab's maximum-severity flaw. Ruby says any application that compiles a regular expression from text an attacker controls is affected, not just GitLab. Version four point zero point eight is missing one of the fixes, Ruby three point two will not be fixed at all, and setting a regex timeout does not help. Separately, Pillow, the most widely used Python image library, fixed a critical flaw in version thirteen in which an attacker-chosen filename for an EPS image can make Ghostscript run commands. Upload handlers that build filenames from user input are the realistic target.

Two notable breaches. Quest Diagnostics, one of the largest US lab testing companies, took its Quanum Lab Services Manager offline after unauthorised access, and believes some patient data was accessed. It says the incident is unrelated to PHP. And Capcom confirmed that its CAPCOM ID membership service exposed more than eleven point seven million email addresses with login metadata, and that attackers changed the passwords on a hundred and ninety-six accounts.

On the state-sponsored side, Kaspersky has described BlackX, a firmware bootkit derived from BlackLotus that it attributes to the China-linked group APT41, used against diplomats in Central Asia. It defeats Secure Boot on systems that are patched but have not applied Microsoft's certificate revocations, which still have to be done by hand. ESET has detailed how Sandworm took over access from another Russia-aligned group before a destructive attack on a Ukrainian logistics firm last year. And Symantec has linked APT28 to a new backdoor at a naval shipbuilder and a military engine maker in NATO countries. These attributions come from individual vendors.

One more for anyone who uses Tor. A researcher found three hundred and thirty-three exit relays, about a tenth of all exits, redirecting SSH connections to attacker servers to capture passwords from users who clicked past the host key warning. The Tor Project has removed them. That warning, it turns out, is there for a reason.

Now AI security.

SOCRadar has found exposed infrastructure belonging to a Russian-speaking affiliate of the INC and The Gentlemen ransomware groups, running an AI framework it calls Phantom. The structure is described as AI-executed and human-approved. An operator writes a victim-specific prompt and reviews every step, while the intrusion itself runs through the OpenCode agent using commercial models. When a model refused, the operator simply reworded the request as an authorised red team assessment. Targets included FortiClient EMS, SimpleHelp and VPN gateways. This rests on one firm's analysis, but it fits Microsoft's findings on the ConnectWise intrusions earlier this week. Both point the same way: AI agents are taking on the routine work of an intrusion, and the familiar weak points, edge devices and reused passwords, are still where they get in.

The Fortinet release is the defensive mirror image: a vendor using frontier models to find its own bugs, and changing its disclosure process because it assumes attackers have the same tools.

Researchers at V12 found flaws in x402, an open standard that lets AI agents pay for API calls. In older versions, the default client set no spending limit, so a malicious API could ask an agent to sign a payment for its entire token balance. The fixes shipped in August, but without any security advisory, so dependency scanners do not warn anyone still on older versions. If your agents can spend money, give them a limit.

On a related theme, Axios reports, citing people familiar with the matter, that major US banks are detecting unsanctioned third-party AI agents logged into customer accounts with the customer's credentials, some appearing to act on their own. Morgan Stanley has begun blocking them, and others are moving toward read-only access. That one hasn't been confirmed on the record yet.

Briefly, Microsoft Teams will start using AI to detect phishing in external first-contact chats from early November.

In industry news, Proofpoint will acquire Varonis in an all-cash deal worth six point three billion US dollars, combining Proofpoint's email and identity protection with Varonis's data security, and pitching the result as a platform for adopting AI safely. Microsoft has reminded customers that Windows Server twenty sixteen reaches end of support on the twelfth of January, so the clock is running on migration plans. And the UK has proposed up to fourteen years in prison for deliberately damaging undersea cables and pipelines, replacing a law from eighteen eighty-five whose maximum fine is a thousand pounds.

Finally, the outlook and patch priorities.

Critical, today. Apply the Fortinet updates, internet-facing devices first. Finish PHP patching and turn off session upload progress. Patch on-premises Arista VeloCloud Orchestrators, and update or remove the Collabora built-in CODE server on Nextcloud. Both have a CISA deadline of Monday.

High, this week. Patch ArubaOS. Update Ruby and GitLab. Upgrade Pillow. Replace eMudhra certificates before Chrome stops trusting them on the twenty-seventh. And apply the Secure Boot revocations if you have been putting them off.

Watch for the first confirmed PHP to Shell compromise or a reliable public exploit, early analysis of the Fortinet patches ahead of the November disclosure, more victims from the VeloCloud and Collabora exploitation, and whether other Australian organisations named in the Adfection research come forward. Indicators are in the show notes.
