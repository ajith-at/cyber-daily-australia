Good morning from Melbourne on the last Friday of November. This is U51, your cyber and AI security brief for Friday the 27th of November, covering the 24 hours to about six this morning, Melbourne time.

Here are the headlines. 1Password says an attacker used a hijacked employee GitHub account to push malicious code into its backend and copy customer account details, though not vault contents. Flight Centre says a significant number of customer records may have been accessed through a third-party accommodation booking system. Attackers exploited Mitel's MiCollab collaboration server before a patch existed, and US authorities have added it to the exploited list. And Swiss authorities have confirmed the country's first successful cyberattacks carried out with AI agents.

Our lead story is 1Password, and the most important part isn't the data that was copied but the route the attacker took to get it. On the 19th of November, 1Password began investigating signs that someone had taken over an employee's corporate GitHub account. The attacker used that account to reach source code and push malicious code to backend services, which then downloaded account records for a subset of users. Those records include names and email addresses, account and user identifiers, account creation and last sign-in times, public keys, Secure Remote Password parameters, and tokens from some invitation, provisioning and account recovery emails. The attacker also copied private source code and got into the continuous integration and delivery system, the pipeline that builds and ships the software, which holds infrastructure secrets. 1Password says it has rotated or revoked every credential that may have been exposed. Vaults, the items in them, the keys that protect them and account recovery keys were not affected. Because vaults are encrypted on the device, 1Password can't decrypt them, and that's what limited the direct damage. But access to source code and the build pipeline is the kind of foothold that could, in principle, allow malicious changes to backend, web or client software, and tampered client code could read secrets after they're legitimately decrypted. To be clear, there's no evidence that happened, and 1Password says its apps and browser extensions weren't affected. The questions for any software supplier are about controls: GitHub access, branch protection, code review, separation of duties and approvals for production deployments, so that no single identity can write, approve and ship code to production. Developer laptops and accounts need to be treated as likely points of compromise. 1Password has lengthened the codes in invitation and recovery emails from six digits to ten characters. The immediate risk for users is phishing that uses the exposed details, so be wary of unexpected emails, texts or calls about 1Password invitations or account recovery. Organisations should watch 1Password admin, recovery and provisioning activity, confirm that privileged users have phishing-resistant sign-in, and ask 1Password for assurance on its source control and release processes. The company hasn't said how the GitHub account was compromised or how many users are affected.

Australian focus.

Flight Centre Travel Group told the Australian Securities Exchange it is investigating unusual activity in a third-party accommodation booking system used by the Flight Centre brand, Travel Associates, Cruiseabout and some independent agents. The company believes a significant number of customer records may have been accessed, but it doesn't yet know how many were actually taken. Most of those records hold only names, some with basic travel details. A very small percentage may include limited passport details, email addresses, phone numbers, dates of birth and, in rare cases, health or mobility information. Flight bookings and corporate travel run on other systems and aren't affected, and so far there's no sign that card or bank details were accessed. Flight Centre has brought in Mandiant, changed authentication keys and credentials with the supplier, and is notifying authorities. It hasn't named the supplier. With Christmas travel being booked now, expect scammers to send convincing messages about bookings. If you're a customer, don't act on unexpected booking emails or calls; check directly with your agent.

Global threats.

Mitel's MiCollab collaboration platform is the next one to patch. Mitel fixed five flaws in its audio, web and video conferencing component on the 16th of November, and all five can be exploited without logging in. Two are critical, including a command injection rated ten out of ten. The US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency, or CISA, has now added all five to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue. Accenture's APEX Labs found that attackers were already in before the advisory came out, with web shell activity at one victim before 23:00 UTC on the 15th of November, which is 10am on the 16th, Melbourne time. The pace was quick: the first command ran 12 minutes after the first request, the attacker had root within half an hour, and at 32 minutes it used directory credentials stored by MiCollab to log in to a domain controller. About 20 hours later it reached backup and database servers and took data. Accenture says the attacker's server contacted two commercial AI services at the same second it sent its authentication bypass request. Accenture assesses that AI-assisted tooling drove parts of the intrusion, but that is an assessment, not a confirmed finding. A second organisation's server has been talking to the same attacker infrastructure since the 9th of November. Mitel's advisory doesn't mention exploitation. Apply Mitel's patch for version 10.3 or move to a later release, with details in the show notes, take the conferencing component off the internet if you can, and check whether you were compromised before you patched.

The same CISA update added two more products. Vates Xen Orchestra, a management console for XCP-ng virtualisation hosts, has an authentication bypass that gives an attacker administrator rights with a specially crafted login token. Vates says it's being exploited, that a successful attack leaves no log entry, and that compromise can lead to root on the hosts it manages. If it's exposed, update to the fixed version, check the hosts for the indicators in the show notes, and rotate secrets. CISA also added three older flaws in Danfoss store controllers, which are used to run supermarket refrigeration, following yesterday's warning about an unpatched Danfoss bug.

Drupal has released a second fix for its installer. After last week's update, some sites in an unusual or incomplete install state, including production sites, could still be taken over, and Drupal says this is being exploited. Nothing in the admin interface shows whether a site is in that state. Update to the fixed release, run the database update script, lock down the installer, and check logs for changes to the main administrator account. Affected and fixed versions are in the show notes.

StepSecurity has detailed how malware called PolinRider got into open-source build pipelines. On the 19th of October, an account force-pushed rewritten commits carrying the loader into pull requests that were still open at three projects. At AsyncAPI, each malicious commit was replaced within about 40 minutes, leaving a clean final change. At CircuitVerse, the pull request workflow that builds desktop releases ran the loader, and maintainers deleted the 40 installers it published. At AsyncAPI, the loader never ran in the build system, because pull requests from forks need a maintainer's approval before workflows run. StepSecurity found the loader in 576 of 622 repositories it checked. Press reports link PolinRider to North Korea's fake job interview operations, though we haven't seen that independently confirmed. If your CI runs fork pull requests automatically or lets them publish builds, change that.

A Linux kernel flaw nicknamed NFS2ROOT lets anyone who can reach a Linux NFS file server on port 2049 overflow kernel memory without logging in. The security firm OpenSec says its AI tooling turned that into a root shell. The bug dates back to 2014, the exploit hasn't been published, and there are no reports of attacks. Red Hat notes the kernel NFS server isn't switched on by default. If you serve NFS version 4.1 or 4.2, update the kernel and reboot, and limit port 2049 to trusted networks. Fixed kernel versions are in the show notes.

Two breaches to note. FIFA has confirmed unauthorised access to what it calls a limited set of personal and business information. A new group calling itself the Handsome Boy Hacking Club claims it took more than 12 gigabytes and published it after a failed extortion attempt, but those claims aren't verified. And Zodia Custody, Standard Chartered's crypto custody business, says an intruder accessed business contact names and emails for a small number of clients and staff. It reports no unauthorised transactions.

AI security.

Microsoft Threat Intelligence says six threat actors have built the ARTEX agentic framework into their tooling. They include the China-linked Salt Typhoon and Crystal Typhoon, North Korea's Onyx Sleet and Pearl Sleet, and two groups Microsoft tracks as Storm-0940 and Storm-3358. Microsoft says the users include both state-sponsored and financially motivated actors. ARTEX doesn't bring new techniques. It drives familiar penetration testing tools with AI agents at machine speed, and Microsoft says it can go from scanning to privileged access in minutes. In several Storm-0940 intrusions, privileged access came within three hours. In one of its campaigns, Microsoft counted more than 151,000 failed sign-ins, peaking at 2,900 a minute. Attackers change IP addresses and user agents, so Microsoft says behaviour is the reliable signal: bursts of varied requests and retries faster than any person could manage.

Those attacks are now being reported in Europe. Switzerland's National Cyber Security Centre has confirmed the country's first cyberattacks carried out with AI agents, against organisations including critical infrastructure operators in at least two sectors. The centre's director told Swiss public radio there have been five confirmed cases, three aimed at critical infrastructure, and that in two cases the attacks were successful, with no major disruption. Those counts come from a media interview rather than the published notice. Italy's national response team describes agents working in parallel to compromise large numbers of exposed systems within hours. In one case, a coordinator directed five agents from reconnaissance through to persistence. In the cases described, these were attacker-run agent setups exploiting known weaknesses such as guessable passwords, unpatched software and exposed management pages. That's why the Swiss advice is so blunt: patch critical internet-facing systems within 24 hours, cut exposure, and put multi-factor authentication or passkeys on everything reachable from the internet.

Industry.

Britain's National Cyber Action Plan has slipped again, and people familiar with it now expect it on the 9th of December. The government hasn't confirmed the date, and it was first due by the end of last year. And in the US, the head of a Florida ransomware recovery firm has been indicted. Prosecutors allege he claimed to have decryption tools, then quietly paid the ransoms and charged victims a markup. These are allegations and haven't been tested in court.

One late item, which broke after this episode was drafted. South African investment platform EasyEquities, owned by the Johannesburg-listed Purple Group, has restricted client logins and some services after a security incident affecting some of its systems. It says client funds are accounted for and secure, and it is still assessing whether personal information was accessed. Local press notes it is the company's second security scare in under three months, after a breach at an identity verification supplier in September. Clients should treat unexpected calls, emails or messages asking for personal or banking details with caution.

Outlook and patch priorities.

Critical today: Mitel MiCollab, where you should patch, restrict access and look for prior compromise. Xen Orchestra, if it's anywhere near the internet. And Drupal sites, which need the new release and the database update. Next, Danfoss store controllers, and any Linux host serving NFS. For CI pipelines, require approval before workflows run on pull requests from forks. Watch for phishing about 1Password and Flight Centre bookings over the coming days. For US federal agencies, the KEV deadline for the Drupal, Optimizely and Idunno flaws is today, and tomorrow for Mitel, Vates, Danfoss and Juniper. US Thanksgiving may slow vendor updates over the next day or two, but attackers don't take the day off.
