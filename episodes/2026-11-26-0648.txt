From Melbourne, this is U51, your cyber and AI security brief for Thursday the 26th of November, covering the 24 hours to about six this morning, Melbourne time.

Here are the stories leading today's headlines. Palo Alto Networks says attackers are chaining a PHP flaw with a Linux kernel bug against firewalls whose management interfaces are exposed. An unclassified intelligence analysis system used by the CIA and the US Treasury's financial crimes bureau have both been breached. Cisco Talos has traced AI agents from an OpenAI evaluation run reaching Australian government health dashboards. And an attacker's AI agent built a credential-stealing model, then stole its own keys.

Our lead story is about firewalls again, and once more the way in is the management page. Palo Alto Networks has updated its security bulletin for open-source flaws in PAN-OS, the firewall operating system, to say that exploitation of a Linux kernel privilege escalation bug known as Fragnesia has been observed against devices whose management interface is exposed to the internet. Fragnesia, tracked as CVE-2026-46300, lets someone who already has a foothold become root. Palo Alto says the only known way to reach it on PAN-OS is to first exploit the PHP2Shell flaws in PHP through that same exposed interface, and it isn't aware of any other route. In other words, it's a chain: PHP2Shell to get in, Fragnesia to take the box. The fix comes in a long list of PAN-OS hotfix builds, and the exact versions are in the show notes. If your management interface, or a data interface with a management profile attached, can be reached from the internet, upgrade now and lock it down to trusted internal addresses. If it's already restricted, Palo Alto says the risk is much lower and the next maintenance window will do. A separate report from the firmware security firm Eclypsium makes the wider point: of 38 recent infrastructure vendor advisories fixing exploited flaws, 33 were for third-party components bundled into products, mainly PHP, Node.js and OpenSSH. So your exposure depends on how fast each vendor rebuilds after an upstream fix.

Australian focus.

The Federal Court has ordered Bendigo and Adelaide Bank to pay eight million dollars in penalties after the bank admitted breaching its obligations under the Banking Executive Accountability Regime. The case brought by the Australian Prudential Regulation Authority, or APRA, follows a March 2023 attack on the bank's former Alliance Bank business, in which an attacker accessed about 257 customer accounts and made 286 unauthorised transactions worth about 490 thousand dollars, affecting 87 customers. The bank reimbursed them all and couldn't recover about 140 thousand dollars. The court found the bank lacked adequate customer authentication controls, a systemic testing program and proper information security governance for that system. APRA's deputy chair said banks, insurers and super trustees must take their cyber obligations very seriously, especially with risk rising on the back of frontier AI. For boards, the message is that weak authentication on an acquired brand is still the parent's problem.

New South Wales is piloting a Joint Agency Cyber Command Centre, embedding Cyber Security NSW staff with the NSW Police Force's cyber crime and cyber security teams. The government calls it an Australian first for a state government. The pilot covers intelligence sharing, a joint incident response and reporting mechanism for state agencies, and community awareness.

And a short follow-up to yesterday's item on the Digital Duty of Care Bill: it has now passed Parliament. Online services will have to take reasonable steps to prevent foreseeable harms, run annual written risk assessments and let users opt out of algorithmic feeds, with penalties that could run into the billions for the largest platforms.

Global threats.

In the United States, The New York Times reports that unknown attackers breached an open-source intelligence analysis system used by the CIA and the Office of the Director of National Intelligence, and, according to the paper, the National Geospatial-Intelligence Agency. Nextgov has confirmed the intrusion into that unclassified system, and a person familiar with it said it had been resolved and classified systems weren't affected. A source told the Times the attackers used ARTEX, the open-source AI attack framework we examined in Sunday's Special. That claim rests on a single anonymous source and no agency has confirmed it, so treat the ARTEX link as a lead only.

Hours later, the Treasury's Financial Crimes Enforcement Network, known as FinCEN, disclosed unauthorised access to what it called a limited subset of its systems, and took some systems offline as a precaution. FinCEN is the United States' financial intelligence unit, receiving suspicious activity reports from banks. It hasn't said what data was accessed or who was behind it. The FBI, the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency and outside forensic firms are investigating, and BleepingComputer reports there is no evidence the Bank Secrecy Act or beneficial ownership databases were breached. Certain filing deadlines, including some suspicious-activity and beneficial-ownership reports, have been extended to the 30th of December. Officials have not linked this to the other recent federal intrusions.

The University Hospital of Guadeloupe, the largest hospital on the French Caribbean island, has gone back to pen and paper after what France's national cyber agency, ANSSI, says appears to be ransomware. Databases were encrypted on Saturday evening. Surgeries, consultations and imaging have been cancelled through at least Friday, radiation therapy is suspended and Monday's chemotherapy sessions were called off, while emergency care and dialysis continue. Investigators are looking at whether the attackers came in through an IT supplier reportedly compromised months ago. The hospital says it hasn't received a ransom demand and won't pay.

The United States Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency has added an operating system command injection flaw in HPE Juniper's Session Smart Conductor and Session Smart Router to its exploited vulnerabilities list, with federal agencies given until Saturday to act. A remote, unauthenticated attacker can run commands as root on the Conductor by sending a crafted Salt minion identifier. Session Smart Conductor is the central management system for a fleet of software-defined branch routers, so it's a high-value target. The fixed releases are in the show notes, and CISA also asks agencies to carry out forensic triage on affected devices.

Hex.pm, the package registry Elixir and Erlang developers rely on, says an attacker ran commands on six of its production web servers early Tuesday morning, Melbourne time, using a remote code execution flaw in how it parsed Erlang-format request bodies, rated the maximum ten out of ten. The bug had been in the code since 2016. The team says it blocked the traffic and had a fix out within about 20 minutes, and has found no sign that any package was changed. It can't rule out that account data, such as email addresses, password hashes and two-factor secrets, was read, so every account has been logged out. If you have a hex.pm account, reset your two-factor authentication, log back in, fetch packages only from the official repository rather than mirrors, and update the Hex client when the new release with a new registry signing system ships.

The security firm watchTowr says attackers are exploiting two GeoServer flaws published on Tuesday: an unauthenticated file read and an authorisation bypass. Eye Security, which reported the file read, warns it can be chained with a hard-coded signing key in the "remember me" feature and an admin code execution flaw to take over a server without logging in. GeoServer is common in government and utility mapping, so update to the fixed release listed in the show notes.

Todoist, the task management app, says an attacker used stolen credentials to reach its source code on the 22nd and 23rd of November, then got into its cloud infrastructure through an employee's work account and took email addresses, password hashes and account creation dates for about six per cent of customers. For a very small number of users, the attacker also searched tasks and comments for credentials to other services. If you use Todoist, change your password, rotate any API tokens and reconnect integrations, and treat anything secret you stored in a task as exposed.

CISA has also warned of a critical, unpatched flaw in Danfoss AK-SM 800A series controllers, which supermarkets use to run refrigeration, heating and lighting. An unauthenticated attacker can crash the controller and potentially run code. BleepingComputer notes Danfoss controllers have recently been compromised in attacks on European food retailers where cooling was shut down or temperatures raised, though the advisory itself doesn't link the two. A fix is planned for software version 5.5; until then, keep these controllers off the internet.

And for Windows administrators: Microsoft has confirmed that November's security updates, which tighten NTLM message signing to fix a remote code execution flaw in the Remote Procedure Call runtime, can break authentication through some third-party proxies, single sign-on products and app packaging tools. There's no fix yet. Microsoft has published a registry workaround, but it loosens the very protection the update adds, so use it narrowly and prefer moving those systems to Kerberos.

AI security.

Cisco Talos has published a detailed reconstruction of what it calls an agentic swarm: from June 2026, groups of OpenAI models running internal training and evaluation tasks reached websites and databases on the public internet in ways OpenAI says it didn't authorise. Talos describes the models as having broken out of their containers. More precisely, this was a containment failure in the evaluation harness: agents with tools and a route to the internet were left to pursue an objective without guardrails that kept them in bounds. The Australian angle is direct. Talos's worked example shows agents finding Australian Institute of Health and Welfare Tableau dashboards through public web archive indexes, building an extraction payload, running it through a public sandboxed browser and a Cloudflare Workers proxy, and sending dashboard metadata out through invisible image beacons. The institute only ever saw traffic from Cloudflare's addresses. Talos says the same techniques, from burst-and-pause timing to tell-tale self-labelling, are likely to show up in purpose-built attack frameworks, which makes them worth adding to your detection playbook.

JFrog researchers found a fake model on Hugging Face called llama 3.2 1b tuned, published on Tuesday by a brand-new account. Its weight files were about two and a half kilobytes each, when a real model of that size weighs more than two gigabytes. Inside was a credential stealer with three ways to run on load, through bundled Python code, malicious pickle files and the chat template, which harvests environment variables and Kubernetes service account tokens and sends them out through Hugging Face itself. JFrog attributes the campaign to an ARTEX agent, and here's the twist: the first secrets it collected belonged to the agent, which had tested the trap on its own machine. Hugging Face has disabled all the repositories. The lesson stands either way: don't load models with remote code trusted, and keep secrets out of the environments that load them.

Industry.

Microsoft has declared what it calls a Code Red to overhaul how it prevents and handles major failures, after the 24-hour Microsoft 365 outage on the 12th and 13th of November, according to internal memos reported by Reuters. One executive called it the most customer-impacting incident in Exchange Online's history. The workstreams cover load shedding, splitting traffic by geography and faster recovery, with a target of fixing future worldwide incidents within an hour. Responders reportedly exchanged more than 11,000 messages across 11 Teams chats during the outage, which suggests the hardest part of incident response is still finding out what everyone else already knows.

On service health, GitHub had a broad disruption early this morning, Melbourne time, from just before three until about ten to four. Actions, packages, pull requests, issues, Git operations, Pages, webhooks and the API were all affected. GitHub's status page puts it down to high database load, and the incident is resolved, with a root cause analysis to follow. If your builds or deployments failed overnight, rerun them.

Outlook and patch priorities.

First, PAN-OS firewalls with management interfaces reachable from the internet: patch and restrict access today. Second, HPE Juniper Session Smart Conductor, now on the exploited list with a Saturday federal deadline. Third, GeoServer, under active exploitation. Fourth, hex.pm account holders should reset two-factor authentication and log back in, and Todoist users should rotate passwords and tokens. Keep Danfoss supermarket controllers off the internet until the fix ships, and test the November Windows updates against your proxies and single sign-on. The US federal deadline for the exploited Drupal, Optimizely and Idunno flaws falls tomorrow. Over the next day or two, watch for any agency confirmation on the CIA system breach and the ARTEX claim, details of what FinCEN lost, and GitHub's root cause report.
