Hello from Melbourne at the start of a new week. This is U51, your cyber and AI security brief for Monday the 23rd of November, covering the weekend and the 24 hours to about six this morning, Melbourne time.

Here is today's headline rundown. Router and remote-access maker GL.iNet says attackers abused its cloud management service to plant malware on customers' routers and KVM devices. Researchers say prompts were stolen from inside three AI hosting providers for months, including requests marked never to be stored. The FBI is reportedly investigating a hack of the national crime database used by police across the United States. And a software payments platform and a Kubernetes cloud service have both disclosed breaches that leave customers rotating credentials and watching card statements.

First, the weekend in brief. Saturday's Episode 37 led with attackers exploiting a critical Cisco Expressway flaw for root access, now on the United States cyber agency's exploited list. It also covered Amazon's report that a suspected China-linked operator's AI agents found and used a zero-day in a medical imaging tool, the Department of Agriculture taking systems offline after a security incident, Bell Financial saying some client data was likely accessed, and a change at the top of the White House cyber office.

Sunday's Episode 38 was a Sunday Special, AI Agents Hacking at Machine Speed, which opened with Microsoft confirming that an exploited Entra Connect Health flaw exposed tenant administrator contacts and metadata. Its five takeaways: one, take management and research tools off the internet, and never leave a console reachable without authentication. Two, treat a patch release as the start of a race measured in days, internet-facing systems first. Three, remove the easy wins agents grind through, such as default credentials, unauthenticated databases and inference endpoints, keys that were never retired, and accounts without multi-factor authentication. Four, automate the first response on high-confidence detections and have humans supervise the outcomes. And five, hunt for the noise: long scripted command runs, bursts of credential guessing and the published framework indicators. The full episode and its notes are on u51.org.

Now to this morning's news, and the lead story. GL.iNet, which makes home and travel routers and KVM devices, the boxes that let you control a computer's keyboard, screen and mouse remotely, says an unauthorised party exploited a vulnerability in its cloud remote management service to install malicious software on some customers' devices. The company says it has fixed the cloud flaw and blocked that path, so devices can't be newly infected that way, but the fix does not clean devices that were already hit. It has published a simple check for a disguised process, has released beta firmware for affected KVM models, and said stable security firmware for routers and KVMs would follow on the 22nd of November. GL.iNet hasn't said how many devices were affected, how the attack worked or what the malware does.

Others have filled in some of the gaps, and that detail is not yet confirmed by the vendor. BleepingComputer examined an installer script written specifically for GL.iNet devices, which adds persistence, installs SSH keys, swaps in a doctored network tool to hide its own connections, and carries Chinese-language comments. A consultancy that manages its own fleet found suspicious activity on 152 of 183 routers, including live reverse shells and an older backdoor that may not be related, and points to an unchecked command handler in the cloud client. If you own one of these devices, run the vendor's check, update to the latest security firmware, consider a full reset without restoring old settings, and if it's a KVM, check the computer it controls as well. The exact firmware versions and indicators are in the show notes.

Global threats.

The FBI is reported to be investigating an apparent breach of the National Crime Information Center, the database police across the United States use for warrants, stolen vehicles, missing persons and background checks. CNN first reported it, citing two people briefed on the matter, and Associated Press and ABC News have since cited their own sources. The FBI has declined to comment, and it isn't known how the attackers got in, when, or whether records were taken, so treat the detail as still emerging. It would be the second reported intrusion at the bureau in about two months.

Cleverbridge, an e-commerce platform that sells software and subscriptions on behalf of vendors, says a third party copied payment card data stored on its platform. It detected unauthorised activity on the 4th of November and began notifying people on the 20th. One notice seen by BleepingComputer lists the card number, expiry date and the security code on the back of the card, which is not something a payment platform would normally keep. But another notice says the security code wasn't copied while contact details were, so the exact data differs by customer. Cleverbridge says it wasn't ransomware and hasn't named the affected merchants, though Malwarebytes says a small subset of its own customers were involved. If you've bought software through a Cleverbridge checkout, check your card statements.

Rackspace says an intruder used leaked credentials on the 17th of November to get into parts of Rackspace Spot, its managed Kubernetes service. For most of that day the intruder could act as legitimate users in some customer organisations, start workloads, list nodes and volumes, and read Kubernetes Secrets, which is where teams keep database passwords, cloud keys and other credentials. Rackspace announced that service was fully restored on the 21st, after an extended console outage. This rests on a customer notice reported by BleepingComputer, and Rackspace hasn't said how many customers were affected. If you use Spot, reissue your tokens, download fresh configuration files, rotate every credential stored in your Secrets at the source, and check logs in the systems those credentials unlock. Indicators are in the show notes.

Two patches for anyone who processes files or email. The first is Ghostscript, the interpreter many Linux servers quietly use to render PDFs, PostScript and images. Researchers at pwn.ai showed that a crafted file can switch off its safety sandbox and run commands on the server, and reached it through ordinary upload, thumbnail, print and webmail features in about ten applications, some needing no login at all. The fix shipped in September, and no attacks have been reported, but several distributions still listed it as vulnerable or pending last week, and copies bundled inside containers or applications won't be fixed by updating the host. A useful reminder that "we don't use Ghostscript" is often untrue.

The second is GPGME, the library programs use to talk to GnuPG encryption. A malicious OpenPGP key with an unusually long user identifier can trigger a memory bug that the project says could be used to steal data, and it hasn't ruled out code execution. It affects most mail clients, many chat clients, Samba and Gpg4win. No attacks are known. Update the library and any application that bundles it; the affected and fixed versions are in the show notes.

Palo Alto Networks says two critical flaws in the NGINX web server code, rated 9.2, also reach its PAN-OS firewalls and Panorama when the HTTP/2 option is switched on, and could let an unauthenticated attacker crash a worker process or run code. Palo Alto isn't aware of any exploitation and rates the urgency as moderate, since the attack is complex to pull off. Hotfixes are out for several branches, with more due today, United States time, and turning HTTP/2 off removes the exposure in the meantime. The fixed builds are in the show notes.

And a quick note on deadlines. The four flaws the United States cyber agency added to its exploited list on Friday, in Cisco Expressway, VMware vCenter, SAP NetWeaver and Apache Roller, reach their federal patch deadline today, United States time.

AI security.

The weekend's most uncomfortable AI story is about where your prompts actually go. Research published by the industrial security firm Dragos, written by HD Moore, planted about two million prompts carrying unique links and fake credentials across AI services between July and November and watched who used them. It found attackers inside three AI inference providers, the earliest from mid-July. They were copying traffic, including requests flagged for Zero Data Retention, the setting that is supposed to mean a prompt is never stored. Some planted Amazon Web Services keys were tested within hours, with attempts to dig through secrets and create new users to keep access, and on the 1st of November the attacker used the ARTEX agent framework we discussed on Sunday to check which keys still worked. The research also describes marketplace sellers logging the requests they served, and an unnamed Chinese AI lab fetching links from more than half of the prompts sent to its models.

OpenRouter, a gateway that routes customer requests to many providers, says it has removed three providers, taken 125 endpoints off its Zero Data Retention list and notified potentially affected customers, and that it was not itself breached. The researchers haven't named the three breached providers, so details are still emerging. Separately, Phala says requests to one model between the 21st of October and the 8th of November should be treated as exposed, and Together AI says an AI coding agent committed a Hugging Face credential to its public GitHub repository, and someone used it to download stored prompt samples. Five of the credentials that attacker then tested turned out to be Dragos's own canaries, which is one way to announce yourself. The lesson for teams: a privacy flag only binds the first hop. Choose your upstream providers deliberately, keep secrets out of prompts, and consider planting your own canary credential, treating any use of it as an incident.

Researchers at NebuSec published a laboratory demonstration against Meta's Muse personal agent, and say every weakness has been fixed, though Meta hasn't commented publicly. Getting the agent to export a malicious web page gave them code execution inside its container through a browser flaw. Weaknesses in how Muse's outbound monitor classified requests meant chat history and stored memories could be sent out without an approval prompt, and altered too. A kernel exploit then broke out of the container to the user's own virtual machine, where credential storage sat. Nothing here involved the model acting on its own. It's a harness whose isolation and egress rules had gaps, and a good checklist for anyone building agents: check what outbound traffic carries before treating it as harmless, and don't rely on a container as the only wall around credentials.

On the defensive side, Signal will now restrict screenshots by default to stop operating system AI assistants on iOS and Android from reading its chats. Signal itself calls the protection incomplete, since agents can reach data in other ways, and is asking Apple and Google for a documented way for apps to opt out of AI data access entirely.

Closer to home.

The Barefoot Investor, Scott Pape, says scammers have used an AI clone of his voice to impersonate him on Facebook and tout fake investment schemes. He found out when a mate texted to say he'd just been talking to him there. No victim numbers have been reported. His own fixes are worth copying: move two-factor codes from text messages to an authenticator app, let a password manager create your passwords, and agree on a family code word for calls that sound right but feel wrong.

Industry.

A quiet weekend for industry news beyond the breaches already covered, which, given the past fortnight, nobody is complaining about.

Outlook and patch priorities.

First, GL.iNet owners: run the check, install the security firmware, and inspect any computer connected to a KVM. Second, find every copy of Ghostscript in your upload, preview and print pipelines, including containers, and update it, then update GPGME and anything that bundles it. Palo Alto customers running HTTP/2 should apply the hotfixes or switch it off. Third, Rackspace Spot customers should rotate everything in their Secrets, and Cleverbridge customers should watch their cards. And federal agencies in the United States have today's deadline on the four exploited flaws from Friday. Over the next two days, watch for GL.iNet's root cause and device count, an FBI statement on the crime database, the names of the breached AI providers, and which software vendors' customers were caught up at Cleverbridge.
