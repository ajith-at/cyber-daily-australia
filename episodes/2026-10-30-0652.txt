Good morning from Melbourne. This is U51, your cyber and AI security brief for Friday the thirtieth of October, twenty twenty-six, covering the twenty-four hours to quarter to six this morning, Melbourne time.

Palo Alto Networks says attackers are now using PHP to Shell against its firewalls' management interfaces. Cisco has patched a firewall VPN flaw that attackers are already using to crash devices. A Linux kernel flaw rated nine point eight still has no fix on Ubuntu or Red Hat. And a China-linked group has been stealing encryption keys from inside Cisco's SD-WAN controllers.

First, at home. The RMS Cloud breach is reaching further. The ABC reports that guests of Tasmanian hotels, including Federal Group's Wrest Point casino in Hobart, ALH's Nightcap and Nightelier properties, and Discovery Parks, have received scam emails quoting their real booking number, room type and stay dates. The message says the card used for the booking has been blocked and asks the guest to verify it. RMS's chief executive has apologised, says payment details were not accessed, and says forensic specialists are investigating. RMS is also issuing new credentials to its integration partners. Scamwatch has warned that a message containing your booking details is not proof that it's genuine.

If you've booked a summer stay anywhere, treat a payment request about it with suspicion and call the property on a number you already trust. If you run accommodation on RMS, rotate any integration credentials you've been sent, and tell your guests how you'll genuinely contact them.

Now to PHP to Shell, and yesterday's open question about which firewall vendor was involved. Palo Alto Networks has updated its advisory to say attackers are actively exploiting the two PHP to Shell flaws against PAN-OS management interfaces exposed to the internet. That covers the web interface and the XML programming interface, and the data plane only if a management profile is attached to it. Palo Alto told The Hacker News it is aware of Dream's report of twenty-five rooted firewalls, three of them in a US government-affiliated network. The company hasn't said in so many words that it is the unnamed vendor, though the timing points that way.

Fixes are out for many PAN-OS versions, but some hotfixes are only due today or tomorrow, US time. Until yours arrives, Palo Alto's advice is to cut internet access to the management interface, restrict it to trusted internal addresses, and switch on threat prevention for management traffic. The company says fewer than one in ten thousand of its installed firewalls have management exposed to the internet, and that it has contacted each affected customer. That's a small number, but an attacker holding a list of nineteen thousand exposed firewalls only needs a few.

CISA, the US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency, has added Fragnesia, the Linux kernel flaw used in that chain to get from a web shell to root, to its known exploited list. US federal agencies have until tomorrow, the thirty-first, which tells you how CISA rates it. Because these appliances are common across Australian government and critical infrastructure, and the Australian Signals Directorate already flagged the PHP flaws as critical last week, treat this as directly relevant here.

Staying with firewalls, Cisco has patched a high-severity flaw in its Secure Firewall ASA and Firewall Threat Defense software that is already being exploited. An attacker without credentials can send a crafted request to the SSL remote access VPN and make the device reload. It only bites where secondary authentication is configured on a tunnel group, and that includes the default group, which is easy to overlook. Cisco hasn't said who is behind it or how widely. A firewall that keeps rebooting is an outage at best and a distraction at worst, so patch it, or disable secondary authentication until you can.

Cisco Talos has also described a group it tracks as UAT eleven four seven three, which exploited the maximum-severity Catalyst SD-WAN controller flaw patched back in February. It installed a custom framework called KeyDrift that runs inside the controller's own processes, reads control traffic after it has been decrypted, and pulls out what Talos assesses to be the keys protecting traffic between sites. Targets were space and satellite services, defence and aerospace, and policy research organisations. Talos assesses with high confidence that the group's infrastructure overlaps heavily with Silent Train, the hacked-device network Lumen says Volt Typhoon used, which we covered yesterday. That doesn't make this group Volt Typhoon, because these networks are shared. Talos also describes the attacks as historical. If you patched in February, check the controllers anyway, because a patch doesn't remove an implant that was already there.

And CISA has added a VMware ESX logging flaw to its list after CrowdStrike reported that the China-nexus group Warp Panda used it as a zero-day early this year to hide its activity from audit logs, in attacks on the US legal sector. Its CVSS score is only two point seven. Warp Panda pairs it with session token theft and registering its own multi-factor devices on compromised accounts, so a password reset alone won't evict it.

Next, a follow-up on the Linux exploit Anthropic described earlier this week. The underlying bug is a race in how the kernel sets up IPv4 TCP connections, and the kernel's own CVE team rates it nine point eight. As of yesterday, Ubuntu and Red Hat Enterprise Linux had no fixed kernel, and Red Hat lists its fix as deferred. Debian, Oracle Linux and some SUSE and Amazon Linux kernels are fixed. Vendor scores range from five point nine to eight point one, because the race is hard to win. Anthropic says its test took forty-eight minutes to leak a kernel address and five more to get a root shell, on a server with a multipath TCP listener. No attacks have been reported. Install fixed kernels where they exist, and watch for Ubuntu and Red Hat releases.

Briefly from elsewhere. Belgium's finance ministry has taken its MyMinfin, Tax-on-web and Biztax portals offline after an intrusion on Tuesday night. It says it has no sign that data was taken, and it has pushed this weekend's filing deadline out to the sixteenth of November, which I suspect a few Belgian accountants won't complain about.

Japan Airlines says Amadeus, which runs its reservations, ticketing and check-in system, found that reservation data for about ninety-two thousand customers had been accessed, including passport numbers for about two thousand. It's the latest in a run of Japanese breaches that has the Prime Minister promising emergency measures.

And the security start-up NebuSec has disclosed a Telegram for Android flaw, fixed in September, that could be chained to take over a phone once the victim answered a voice call. No use in the wild has been reported, and the details come from that one firm. Check your app is up to date.

On to AI security. Check Point has found a campaign it calls Yoba that offers fake desktop apps for ChatGPT and Claude, under made-up model names, through GitHub pages pushed up the search results. The download bundles a genuine signed OpenAI or Anthropic executable with a malicious library that the signed program loads, and the end result is a cryptocurrency miner. A valid signature only tells you who wrote that one file, not what it loads. Get AI tools from the vendor's own site or your organisation's software portal.

An independent researcher reports twenty-six thousand four hundred working credentials, most of them cloud and AI service keys, in public repositories on Gitee, the Chinese code hosting service. Over nine thousand were Alibaba Cloud keys. He told the main vendors, but hasn't confirmed any were revoked. If a key of yours ever touched a public repository, rotate it.

And Google has released Gemini 4 Carbon, claiming leading scores for finding, exploiting and patching vulnerabilities. Those are the company's own benchmark figures. Taken with Anthropic's results this week, the tools are getting better at both ends of the job.

In industry news, GitHub has a package firewall in public preview for Enterprise Cloud customers. It checks npm packages at install time, partly to stop AI agents and build runners pulling in malware without anyone noticing. And Alpine Linux has a warning for anyone running GitLab on their own Ruby. Updating GitLab didn't fix the critical regular expression flaw on its server, because the fix lives in GitLab's patched Ruby. After an attempted attack, Alpine rebuilt the server and reset every password. If you build GitLab yourself, update Ruby too.

One late item, which landed after this episode was first written. CISA has added two of today's stories to its known exploited list: the Cisco ASA and Firewall Threat Defense VPN flaw, and the GitLab regular expression flaw behind the Alpine attempt. Both carry a deadline of the first of November for US federal agencies, and for GitLab CISA also asks for forensic triage, so look for signs of compromise as well as patching.

Your priorities for the next forty-eight hours. Critical: Palo Alto PAN-OS PHP to Shell fixes, and take every firewall management interface off the internet today. Critical too: Linux kernel updates for Fragnesia. High: Cisco ASA and Firewall Threat Defense, GitLab and the Ruby it runs on, Cisco SD-WAN controllers, including a check for implants, VMware ESX, and distribution kernels for the TCP race as they land. And if you're an RMS customer, rotate those integration credentials.

Looking ahead, expect the remaining PAN-OS hotfixes, more appliance vendors confirming whether their firmware bundles vulnerable PHP, possible Ubuntu and Red Hat kernel releases, and more RMS-themed scams as people book for summer.
