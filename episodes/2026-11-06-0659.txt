Good morning from Melbourne. You're listening to U51, the cyber and AI security brief for Friday the 6th of November, covering the 24 hours to about a quarter to six this morning, Melbourne time.

Here are the headlines. OpenAI has named the model behind its mid-October Linux kernel research as GPT-6 Cyber, after an autonomous pipeline built nearly a thousand working Linux root exploits and candidate patches as defensive work reviewed by humans before anything went to maintainers. Singapore's cyber agency warns that a critical Metabase flaw is under active attack. VulnCheck has published a ten-flaw chain that turns Control Web Panel into unauthenticated root. Australia's cyber centre has new guidance on running open-weight AI models securely. And Japan is seeking earlier access to undisclosed vulnerability findings from United States AI firms.

We start with OpenAI's research note, titled A thousand fewer paths to root, and this morning's related announcement of GPT-6 Cyber. The company describes a five-stage autonomous pipeline powered by that model: agents pick security-relevant parts of the Linux kernel that a low-privileged user can reach, scan for candidates, reproduce bugs in isolated virtual machines, build exploits, write candidate patches, and then a human security researcher reviews the package before anything is reported to maintainers. In a mid-October run against a stock desktop Linux distribution, with the usual protections switched on, the pipeline analysed more than two thousand three hundred candidate findings, dynamically reproduced one thousand seven hundred and twenty-four vulnerabilities, and produced nine hundred and forty-eight distinct local privilege escalation exploits, each with a candidate patch. The run cost about one point two million US dollars in API credits, or roughly one thousand two hundred and seventy-nine dollars per exploit. OpenAI also says that in a sample of one hundred and sixty-six exploits it checked, the agents used ten new techniques, the details of which it is withholding. OpenAI presents the work as defensive research aimed at finding and fixing flaws before attackers do. Two caveats matter on air. First, OpenAI says it has disclosed three hundred and four critical findings with candidate patches so far and is working with Patch the Planet partners on the rest, so do not hear nine hundred and forty-eight as nine hundred and forty-eight public disclosures. Second, within ten days while the scan was running, sixty-one of those local privilege escalations had already been patched upstream by other contributors, with more in staging or under public discussion. Patches still have to land, ship in distributions, and get installed. OpenAI's own takeaway is blunt enough without hype: for a well-resourced actor, a low-privileged account on an operating system that is not up to date is functionally close to root, which is why sandboxes and isolation still matter, and why automated patch generation has to keep pace with automated discovery.

Late this morning, OpenAI confirmed that GPT-6 Cyber is the model behind that run. Access is initially limited to selected approved Daybreak Red customers through the API and Codex, with identity verification and controls such as single sign-on, multi-factor authentication, managed devices and IP allowlisting, and it expands to all Daybreak Red customers in coming weeks. OpenAI rates the model Critical for cyber under its Preparedness Framework, which is why access is gated. Separately, it announced a one billion US dollar Daybreak for Frontline Defenders grant programme for resource-constrained teams such as government and essential services. OpenAI also says the model found eighty-one new high-severity Firefox bugs by Mozilla's rating, and Mozilla has begun shipping fixes.

Australian focus.

Australia's cyber centre has published two new guides on open-weight AI models. One explains the opportunities and the cyber and supply-chain risks that come with publicly released model weights. The other covers how to select, deploy and operate those models securely, including governance, provenance, monitoring, maintenance and incident response. Hosting a model yourself can mean more control over data and operations, but you also take on more of the security burden across the model's life. Separately, with the United States Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency and the National Security Agency, it has refreshed a short list of post-quantum questions organisations should put to their vendors as cryptographic migration planning gets real. Neither package is a breach story; both are practical guidance for operators.

Global threats.

Singapore's Cyber Security Agency has warned that a critical Metabase vulnerability is being actively exploited. The flaw is an unauthenticated SQL injection in the password-reset interface, rated ten out of ten, and a proof of concept is public. A successful attack can hand the attacker administrator access to the Metabase instance, from which they can change configuration, steal credentials for connected databases, and read or export data those connections can reach. Metabase first disclosed the issue in August after a cloud zero-day attack; cloud instances were patched then. Singapore's four November alert is about active exploitation now, especially unpatched self-hosted instances. Self-hosted deployments that are still on vulnerable builds need to move to the fixed point releases now. The affected and fixed versions are in the show notes. If you cannot upgrade immediately, block the password-reset API endpoint as a temporary workaround, and after you upgrade, revoke sessions, review administrator accounts and API keys, rotate database credentials, and check logs for the attack pattern Metabase has described.

VulnCheck has disclosed ten vulnerabilities in Control Web Panel, the hosting control panel formerly known as CentOS Web Panel. Chained together, they take an unauthenticated attacker from the user panel to code execution as a hosting account and then to root through a root-owned loopback API. The entry is almost comic: the login page issues a redirect that never actually stops execution, so an attacker-controlled module name still gets included. VulnCheck has built a working exploit for its customers, and the technical write-up is detailed enough that others can follow. VulnCheck's scan data puts the exposed user-panel surface in the tens of thousands of hosts, with the usual caveat that banner counts are an order of magnitude, not a census. No in-the-wild exploitation of this exact chain has been confirmed yet, but earlier Control Web Panel remote code execution was mass-exploited soon after public exploits appeared, so treat this as urgent. Fixed builds start at version 1.29, with 1.30 current at disclosure. Upgrade, and keep the admin and user panels off the public internet wherever you can.

A short follow-up on yesterday's phone watering-hole story. CISA has added an older Apple flaw to its known exploited vulnerabilities catalogue. It is an improper authentication issue that can let an attacker who already has powerful read and write capability bypass Pointer Authentication. Apple fixed it in May 2024, including Safari 17.5 and iOS 17.5. United States federal agencies have until the 18th of November. Press coverage links the listing to the same Russia-linked Poisoned Source campaign we covered yesterday, so this is a catalogue update, not a new campaign. If your Apple devices are years behind on updates, you already had a reason to catch up.

Apache OFBiz has shipped a large security release. All versions before the fixed build are affected, and the set includes pre-authentication remote code execution among many other issues. The fixed version is in the show notes. If you run OFBiz for ERP or e-commerce workflows, this is a priority upgrade, not a tidy-up.

And hosting provider DreamHost says an attacker reached the server behind its web-based File Manager. Users who logged into that File Manager between roughly August and the 23rd of October should treat their FTP and SFTP passwords as compromised. A limited set of webmail credentials was also exposed, and DreamHost reset affected passwords. The company says it has found no evidence that control-panel logins or billing card details were accessed. Watch for phishing that pretends to be DreamHost asking for a password; the real reset path is inside the control panel.

AI security.

OpenAI's defensive pipeline and GPT-6 Cyber announcement are the lead for this section as well as the episode, and they land beside a broader warning from the United Kingdom's National Cyber Security Centre. In a new assessment on artificial intelligence and cyber intrusion, the centre's baseline view toward 2028 is that AI-powered tools have almost certainly increased zero-day discovery, while defensive AI can shorten the window from zero-day to a known, patchable flaw, but only if organisations actually configure the tools and speed up their own patch and testing processes. OpenAI is describing the same gap from the other side: discovery is scaling faster than human triage unless patch generation scales with it.

Japan, meanwhile, plans to seek earlier access to information on undisclosed software vulnerabilities from United States AI developers and other overseas technology firms, according to a Nikkei report carried by iTnews. An emergency cybersecurity package could go to a ministerial meeting as early as today. The push follows the recent wave of attacks after critical PHP flaws were disclosed, and it sits alongside Japan's interest in wider access to advanced AI models. Treat the reporting as a single-source lead until the package is published.

Industry developments.

Beyond the Australian guidance already covered, the day's industry signal is that governments are rearranging how vulnerability information moves when AI labs can find bugs at this pace. Japan's early-access ask and the open-weight and post-quantum publications from Australia's cyber centre both push in that direction: defenders need earlier, clearer information, and operators who run powerful models themselves need clearer duties.

Outlook and patch priorities.

Critical: self-hosted Metabase on vulnerable builds, now under active exploitation; Control Web Panel at 1.29 or later; Apache OFBiz to the fixed release in the show notes.

High: Apple devices that still pre-date the May 2024 fixes tied to the new KEV entry; Linux kernels and distributions kept current as both offensive and defensive discovery accelerate; DreamHost File Manager users rotating FTP, SFTP and any reused passwords.

CISA's only new catalogue addition in this window was the Apple flaw. Over the next day or two, watch for Japan's ministerial package, further Metabase victim reports, scanning against Control Web Panel after the detailed public write-up, and any update on how many of OpenAI's critical kernel reports have landed upstream.
