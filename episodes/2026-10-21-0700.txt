From Melbourne, this is U51 with your cyber and AI threat brief for Wednesday the twenty-first of October, twenty twenty-six, covering the twenty-four hours to seven o'clock this morning, Melbourne time.

The headlines. The private keys behind eight intermediate certificates from the Indian certificate authority eMudhra have been compromised, prompting emergency blocks in every major browser and an urgent Go release. Palo Alto Networks says the Qilin and INC ransomware groups are using a firewall flaw to get past multi-factor authentication and then go straight for the virtualisation layer. Researchers watched criminals point autonomous AI agents at more than twelve hundred websites, with Australian shops among the targets. And OVHcloud's command-line tool joins the growing list of hijacked software releases.

Australian focus first.

The AI agent story has a local angle, so start there. A researcher at the Norwegian firm NorForge, Snorre Fagerland, went looking for exposed copies of Cairn, an open-source tool built for authorised penetration testing that lets AI agents work step by step towards a goal. He found about two dozen instances being used maliciously, with their control panels left open to the internet, which meant he could read the attackers' work as it happened. The busiest cluster, which NorForge calls A I A zero zero zero one, is Chinese-language and linked to more than eleven hundred attack projects. Its apparent aim is lasting access to online shops and payment systems, with targets in the United States, Japan and Australia. More on the mechanics in the AI section, but for Australian retailers the practical checks are simple: look for unexpected file upload forms in odd directories, and make sure any Magento or Adobe Commerce store has the latest hotfix applied. The Australian victims, if any, haven't been named.

Next, a follow-up on yesterday's AOMEI Backupper story. The company told Recorded Future News that it has not yet identified or notified customers who were infected when attackers tampered with its update server between April and July. Google says the victims it found spanned information technology services, telecommunications and higher education in almost a dozen countries, including Australia, and that the true number is probably higher. AOMEI has released a free detection tool. If its software is anywhere in your estate, hunting across the whole April to July period remains the advice, because the server fix does nothing for machines already infected.

ConnectWise, whose cloud services are widely used by Australian managed service providers, says it has reset accounts, replaced application credentials and tightened administrative access, and its Cloud Backup, software as a service security and cloud management products remain switched off. The security firm Huntress has now seen a second customer Microsoft three six five tenant where the compromised application granted itself global administrator rights and planted new secrets on other applications for persistence. ConnectWise is clear that its own monitoring doesn't tell you whether your tenant was touched, and that affected applications should not be reconnected until it issues formal instructions.

One more local item. Research from the identity security firm Semperis, reported by Cyber Daily, says ransomware crews increasingly use psychological or physical threats against staff, and that in fifty-three per cent of the Australian cases it surveyed, victims said the attackers followed through. That figure is self-reported by survey respondents, but it is a fair prompt to make sure incident response plans cover people's safety, not only server restoration.

Now to global threat activity.

The most consequential story overnight is about trust itself. Mozilla and Google's Chrome Root Program confirmed on the twentieth that the private keys for eight eMudhra intermediate certificate authority certificates are compromised. Six of them sign website certificates, and two sign code. Anyone holding one of those keys could issue a certificate for any website that a browser would accept. The problem was reported on the evening of the seventeenth by a researcher who proved the point by signing the message "We have a problem here" with each of the eight keys, which is one way to make sure nobody asks for further evidence. Chrome blocked the website certificates within about two hours, Firefox within about five, and Apple and Microsoft followed on the nineteenth and twentieth. Chrome says no certificate chaining to those intermediates had been logged publicly before its block, so none would have been trusted by browsers that require that logging. Mozilla has removed the affected eMudhra root from its trust store, and Chrome will remove it too.

The catch is everything that isn't a browser. Many programs never check revocation, so they keep trusting a chain until their code or their root bundle changes. Go has shipped versions one point two seven point three and one point two six point ten, which reject any chain containing the eight keys, but only once you rebuild your programs. Python's certifi bundle, Rust's web P K I roots and Homebrew's certificates have all dropped the root. Node.js has no fix yet. How the keys left eMudhra, and whether anyone besides the researcher holds them, is still unknown.

Next, Palo Alto Networks' threat intelligence team, Unit 42, says it continues to see ransomware operators exploiting an authentication bypass in PAN-OS and Prisma Access, patched back in May and already on the United States Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency's known exploited list. The groups named are Qilin and INC. They use the flaw to set up a VPN session that sidesteps multi-factor authentication, then move laterally with remote management tools. The notable shift is what comes next: in one case the attackers went from the VPN gateway to the vCenter management appliance, disabled protections on the ESXi hypervisors, and dumped credentials straight from a domain controller's virtual disk, out of reach of any endpoint agent inside the guest. If you patched in May but never checked for successful gateway connections before then, now would be a good time.

On the software supply chain, OVHcloud says an attacker used a hijacked contributor's GitHub account to swap the download files on five existing releases of its command-line tool for a build that steals secrets from disk. The swap was live for about ninety-four minutes on Monday, and the attacker also replaced the checksum file so the hashes matched. A customer's build pipeline caught it, because it pinned its own hash and started failing, which is about the best advertisement for hash pinning you will see this year. Homebrew users were protected for the same reason. Anyone who installed or updated by script or direct download in that window should rotate every secret on that machine, from a different machine, and then install the clean version zero point one six. OVHcloud has now switched on GitHub's release immutability setting, which would have prevented the swap.

State activity. Symantec has attributed intrusions at two NATO member states to APT28, the Russian military intelligence-linked group also known as Fancy Bear. Targets included a law enforcement agency, a naval shipbuilder and a military aircraft engine maker. At the shipbuilder, operators returned to a compromised machine repeatedly over a week, stole credentials two different ways, opened a persistent SSH tunnel into the internal network and deployed a previously undocumented loader that only decrypts its payload with a key supplied at run time. The latest activity was seen in late September, and it isn't known whether data was taken.

On vulnerabilities. Horizon three dot A I has published a working proof of concept for a critical flaw in Fortinet's FortiSIEM, rated nine point four, which lets an unauthenticated attacker write files and run code through the product's internal query service. Fortinet patched it last week in version seven point five point one. Public exploit code for a security monitoring platform tends to shorten the gap to real attacks, so treat it accordingly. The United States Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency also added six flaws to its known exploited catalogue overnight: the four Plex Media Server flaws and the Siemens Software Center weakness we covered yesterday, and a command injection in SiteGuard Server Edition, a host-based web application firewall popular in Japan, which gives root or system rights. The vendor warns that patching can erase traces of an attack, so download its diagnostic bundle first.

Breaches, briefly. Zeabur, a developer hosting platform, says an attacker used leaked internal credentials to read its production database, exposing about three point two million environment variable records across more than three hundred and twenty thousand accounts. The admin tokens involved had never expired and hadn't been rotated since January twenty twenty-four, which means they outlasted the company's previous breach in August. Zeabur will shut down by the end of the year, so customers should rotate secrets and plan a migration now. In California, the Department of Education has warned that a breach at its testing contractor, Educational Testing Service, exposed records covering every public school student over the last three years, including disability status and program eligibility, after attackers spent nearly four months inside a file transfer server. And Nikkei, the Japanese publisher that owns the Financial Times, disclosed its third breach in a month: about one point eight three million records from a development cloud service, around a hundred and fifty thousand of them identifying.

Finally on threats, Citizen Lab has confirmed a zero-click Pegasus spyware infection on the phone of the rector of the University of Belgrade, a prominent supporter of Serbia's student protest movement, days before the country's election on the twenty-fifth. Who was behind it has not been established.

Now, AI security.

Back to Cairn. The agents in the main cluster were given short instructions, by one account fewer than a hundred Chinese characters, and then left to work. They tried more than twelve hundred sites and reached websites belonging to more than a hundred companies, according to Reuters, which reviewed the attackers' records and named Deere, SharkNinja and Cummins among them. All three told Reuters the damage was limited. Successful break-ins took just under a day on average, at a reported cost of roughly fifty United States dollars each in model fees. The underlying models are unconfirmed: a worker name points to DeepSeek driven by Claude Code, but neither the researcher nor Reuters has verified that, so treat it as a lead only. Two lessons stand out. Offensive automation is now cheap enough to be routine. And the exposed panels leaked the victims' stolen secrets too, including those of some legitimate penetration testing firms that had left client work on unsecured instances. If your testers use agent tooling, ask where its control panel lives.

On the defensive side, NIST, the National Security Agency and the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency have published ten voluntary practices for preparing for and responding to incidents where AI agents act outside what was intended or authorised, covering preparation, detection, response, recovery and reporting. Microsoft, meanwhile, says AI agents should be treated as insiders, and is adding per-task permissions and a live risk score that can restrict or stop an agent as risk rises. Neither is revolutionary, but both make useful checklists.

The United States Department of Homeland Security has ordered federal civilian agencies to stop using DeepSeek products and remove them, including third-party products and services developed using DeepSeek. Critics say that last phrase is undefined, and that agencies using AI gateways which route requests to the best available model could be using DeepSeek without knowing it.

And a reminder that hidden instructions cut both ways. A data deletion company called Redact found text in WhatsApp's help pages, invisible on screen, instructing its support chatbot not to recommend contacting a data protection regulator and not to cite privacy laws once a user mentioned legal action, even though WhatsApp's own European privacy policy says users can complain to a regulator. WhatsApp told WIRED the text appeared because of a bug and has been removed. Whether the chatbot ever followed it is unknown. It is not a security breach, but it is a neat demonstration that content fed to a model can be steered by whoever writes it, whether that's an attacker or the platform itself.

Industry, briefly. iTnews reports Salesforce is acquiring the customer engagement platform Braze. And Drupal has released fixes for two critical remote code execution flaws in its core Field UI and the Entity List module, exploitable through common contributed modules, some without authentication. Drupal had already warned that exploits might appear within hours or days.

The outlook. Today's priorities are housekeeping on trust and access. Rebuild Go services on the new releases and update certificate bundles across Python, Rust and container images, accepting that some legitimate eMudhra-issued sites will break until they re-issue. If you run Palo Alto GlobalProtect, confirm the May patch and hunt for unexpected gateway sessions and vCenter access since then. Patch FortiSIEM to seven point five point one, and update Drupal sites today. Anyone who touched the OVHcloud tool on Monday should rotate secrets, and Zeabur customers should rotate theirs regardless. ConnectWise partners should keep reviewing tenant application sign-ins and leave affected applications disconnected. Over the next two days, watch for eMudhra's account of how its keys escaped, the Node.js fix, any naming of Cairn victims, and whether Bullhorn determines that data was taken.
