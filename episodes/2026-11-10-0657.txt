From Melbourne, this is U51, your cyber and AI threat brief for Tuesday the 10th of November, covering the 24 hours to about a quarter to six this morning, Melbourne time.

Here is today's headline rundown. An attacker used the data platform Qubole's own trusted access to walk into customers' Amazon Web Services accounts and take their secrets and data. America's cyber agency has added three actively exploited flaws to its must-patch list, including one in an AI model-serving platform. Vanderbilt University has asked its community to shut down university devices during an active cyber incident. A German web host says it was breached through the server that lets customers connect AI assistants. And a Victorian medical group has disclosed unauthorised access affecting patient information.

The cloud security firm Wiz, working with Sentra and Mandiant, says an attacker used credentials for an Amazon Web Services account that the data lake platform Qubole uses to reach its customers, and between the 14th and 26th of October assumed the access roles those customers had created to trust Qubole. Every one of those requests carried the expected External ID, a matching code meant to guard the handshake, and every one succeeded. Inside, the attacker listed resources, read stored secrets, parameters and serverless function settings, used the TruffleHog tool to test the passwords and keys it found against AWS, Microsoft Entra ID and Okta, and downloaded storage data, sometimes whole buckets at once. How the attacker got Qubole's credentials hasn't been disclosed. Most of the roles abused showed no other legitimate vendor use that month, yet the trust stayed in place. The External ID didn't help, because it isn't a secret. Wiz says a simple condition limiting role assumption to the vendor's own network addresses would have blocked every one of the requests it observed. If you have ever connected Qubole, the hunting queries and indicators are in the show notes; more broadly, list every role that trusts an outside account and delete the ones nobody uses.

Australian focus.

Epichealth, which runs four clinics in regional Victoria, including the Medicare Urgent Care Clinic in Warrnambool and clinics in Ocean Grove and Portarlington, said in a notice published last week that an unauthorised third party accessed part of its systems. Its patient notice says the information involved may include names, dates of birth, contact details, Medicare card numbers, Centrelink customer reference numbers, Veteran card numbers and health information. The clinics remain open, and the incident has been reported to the Australian Cyber Security Centre and the privacy commissioner. Separately, Cyber Daily reports that the ransomware group known as The Gentlemen listed Epichealth on its leak site on the 31st of October; the clinic's notice doesn't itself attribute the incident. Patients should expect scam calls and messages that quote real details, and remember that a Medicare number on its own can't be used to get into a Services Australia account.

ANZ says it is implementing what it calls an enterprise AI control plane. At the bank's annual results, chief executive Nuno Matos said it will give visibility and technical control across users, models, agents, tools, costs and platforms, with traceability of decisions, and will set defined limits on what agents can do and stop activity that goes outside them. The bank hasn't said what software it runs on. ANZ is the third of the big four banks to put AI front and centre at results time in little more than a week, and of those presentations, ANZ's put the most weight on controls rather than savings.

Global threats.

CISA, the US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency, added three flaws to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue yesterday, each with a deadline of this Thursday for federal agencies. The first is sshole, the OpenSSH password bypass on Alpine and other musl-based systems we covered on Saturday. The update is that researchers now report successful exploitation and post-exploitation activity, including attempts to read password hashes and SSH keys, changes to authorised key files, and deployment of a command-and-control implant. If you patched but were exposed before that, check authorised key files and services, not just the version number. The second is FreeSWITCH, the open-source telephony platform. A single text-chat SIP message can make the server run its own administrative commands, including operating system commands, and the stock configuration accepts those messages. Its maintainer SignalWire says this has been exploited. Update to the fixed version in the show notes, or turn off SIP chat on profiles that don't need it. The third, GPUStack, is in our AI section.

Vanderbilt University in Nashville announced an active cyber incident on Monday, US time, that it says may affect university devices, systems and cloud services, and asked its community to shut down university devices and disconnect from campus Wi-Fi. The ransomware group BYOD added the university to its leak site the same morning and claims to hold student and staff records; those claims haven't been verified. The group only posted its first victim at the end of September, and it's also claimed attacks on Michigan State University and the University of New Mexico.

In a software supply chain attack on a crypto project, Ethersphere's own release automation was used to publish malicious versions of its bee-js JavaScript library and the Swarm Desktop app. Both steal the wallet key and password that Swarm Desktop stores, and they're built to install a backdoor on Windows, macOS and Linux. The bad releases are gone and a clean desktop update is out, but that doesn't remove a backdoor or recover a key that's already been sent. Anyone who installed the affected versions, listed in the show notes, should treat that machine as compromised and move funds to a new wallet.

Separately, LNbits, an open-source Bitcoin Lightning wallet server, has told every operator to shut down their servers because of a wave of attacks. One operator says freshly created accounts drained about 0.023 bitcoin from his server before he could stop it. On Sunday the developer estimated a fix within one to two days, a window that closes about 1am Wednesday, Melbourne time. As of the latest check, the project's release page still showed no fixed version, so watch for an official release and restart guidance.

And in research published last week, Fortinet reports that Bitter, a long-running South Asian espionage group, is using a new remote access trojan it calls PapRAT, in both C++ and Rust versions, delivered through shortcut files that abuse the Windows FTP utility and through disk-image attachments.

AI security.

GPUStack is an open-source cluster manager for serving AI models on GPUs, and the third of yesterday's new entries on CISA's list. Before the fixed version, it treated any request that appeared to come from the machine itself as the built-in administrator, and that was the default. Put it behind a reverse proxy on the same host, and every request from the internet looks local. The flaw lets attackers create persistent administrator keys and read worker and cloud-provider secrets. The vendor reports an exploited deployment where custom inference backends ran reverse shells. Upgrading closes the hole, but an administrator key the attacker already made keeps working, so review users, keys and backends, and rotate secrets. AI serving infrastructure has very good GPUs and, it turns out, very trusting front doors.

Mittwald, a German web host for agencies, says an attacker broke in through its MCP server, the Model Context Protocol service that lets customers connect AI assistants to its management platform. According to the company's timeline, the attacker first tested the flaw on the 21st of October, exploited it on the 27th, and wasn't spotted until the 4th of November, by which time 56 customer projects had been compromised. Mittwald shut the MCP server down and says it has found no further compromised projects. It believes the attacker used AI tools because steps followed each other within seconds, but has offered no other evidence, and it hasn't said whether the flaw was in its own code or in open-source libraries; a fuller account is promised this week. Whoever or whatever typed the commands, an AI integration endpoint is an internet-facing service with real privileges, and it needs the same review, monitoring and limits as any other.

In research published last week, Acronis analysed what it calls BOSSAI, a twelve-agent setup, nine of them active when researchers examined it, run by a single attacker they place, with high confidence, in Hualien City in Taiwan. The person aimed the agents at hundreds of organisations in at least 11 countries and got data or access at about ten of them in Taiwan, Mexico and Thailand, including emergency dispatch case records covering seven Taipei districts, pulled from an exposed interface. Commercial AI models mostly refused direct attack requests, so the attack agents ran on locally hosted models with their safety training stripped out. The results were uneven: one scanning tool invented vulnerabilities, more than half of its high and critical findings were wrong, and in the attacks observed, the agents failed wherever logins were enforced. The automation made it cheap to try many targets, but what it reached was data that was already poorly protected.

Industry and research.

The researcher Paulos Yibelo of pwn.ai has published a second instalment on PwnEscape, a critical Linux kernel networking flaw disclosed in mid-October, showing it turned into a full escape from a Firecracker micro virtual machine to root on the host. The test ran Firecracker as root without its jailer, which isn't how it's meant to be deployed, so it doesn't show the same result against a hardened setup. Amazon says its own EC2, Lambda and Fargate services aren't affected. Anyone running their own KVM hosts, Firecracker included, needs to patch the host kernel and reboot, because nothing inside a guest can fix it; affected kernels are listed in the show notes.

Outlook and patch priorities.

Microsoft's November Patch Tuesday lands early tomorrow morning, Melbourne time, so expect a busy Wednesday. Before then, the critical items are GPUStack and FreeSWITCH updates, with the key and backend reviews that go with them, and finishing the sshole work by checking for planted keys and services. Qubole customers, and frankly anyone with old vendor roles, should audit cross-account trust today. Treat MCP servers and other AI integration endpoints as exposed services in your next review cycle. Expect more claims from BYOD as Vanderbilt's response continues, and watch for an official LNbits fixed release.
