From Melbourne on this Halloween Saturday, it's U51, your cyber and AI security brief for the thirty-first of October, twenty twenty-six, covering the twenty-four hours to quarter to six this morning, Melbourne time.

A breach at Binance has hit about six point three billion US dollars in assets, in what analysts call the largest cryptocurrency theft on record. Ivanti has closed the last gaps in an exploited VPN attack chain, and US agencies have until Monday to act. A tampered copy of the Claude Code extension reached up to five thousand developers through Cursor's extension marketplace. And attackers are working hard on fresh Drupal and WordPress flaws.

First, at home. iTnews reports that the Department of Defence has been told to reduce its use of VMware, under what its sources call a mandate issued around September last year, ahead of a licence bill that doubled. A renewal worth a hundred and seventy-eight million dollars was revealed months later. Cost isn't said to be the only reason. Sovereignty has also come up as a concern. Defence declined to comment, and the report rests on unnamed sources, so the scope and formality of the mandate aren't clear. The security point for any organisation going the same way is that a large virtualisation migration means two estates running side by side for a while, and staff learning a new platform under pressure. That's the stretch where misconfigurations creep in, so plan the security work into the migration rather than after it.

The rest of today's main stories touch products that are common across Australian government, universities and business, so treat them as local too.

Now to Binance. The exchange says a security incident on Thursday, US time, affected about six point three billion US dollars in assets, and that close to three billion has been frozen on the blockchain so far. Its early finding is that an attacker compromised a DevOps engineer's laptop, reached a service container, and used it to submit withdrawals that weren't authorised. Binance says it has seen no sign that a wallet's private key leaked, and it will cover all losses. Its co-chief executive added that this was not a single one-click action, and that further points of breach have been found.

The blockchain analysis firm TRM Labs attributes the theft to TraderTraitor, the North Korean state-backed group behind the one and a half billion dollar Bybit theft in February last year, and Chainalysis also links it to North Korea. Binance itself hasn't named anyone, so for now that attribution comes from the analytics firms, not the victim or a government. TRM says preparation started at least thirty-six hours before the theft. The attacker had fifty authorisations signed in advance by Binance's hot wallet, so that at least one would still be valid at the moment it ran, and then emptied the wallet in a single transaction. Elliptic's reading is that the attacker didn't steal Binance's keys. It got Binance's own signing systems to sign for it. That mirrors Bybit, where the compromise was in the people and systems around the wallet, not the cryptography. If you run any system that signs high-value transactions, the engineers' laptops and build infrastructure that can reach the signer deserve the same protection as the keys.

Next, a significant update on Ivanti Connect Secure. Ivanti has now patched the two remaining flaws in the chain that the security firm Volexity found being exploited earlier this month. Together, the three bugs give an attacker without a password root access on the VPN appliance. CISA, the US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency, added both new flaws to its known exploited list yesterday. For the privilege escalation flaw, US federal agencies have until the second of November and must also check for signs of compromise. Volexity says the attacker used its access to pull credentials stored on the appliance, search Active Directory for local administrator passwords and BitLocker recovery keys, and tamper with logs. So upgrade to the fixed versions listed in the show notes, and if your appliance was exposed this month, assume someone looked and go and check.

Drupal has warned that a highly critical flaw in its Commerce Core module is being exploited. It's an access bypass that needs no login, and Drupal says it may have a financial impact on the store, which in plain terms means orders and payments. The module runs on more than forty-two thousand sites. Drupal hasn't described the attacks. The fix is Commerce Core version three point three point thirteen, or eight point x two point four one for older sites, and the same releases fix five lower-rated Commerce issues. Update, and audit recent orders for anything that doesn't add up.

WordPress fixed a different problem on Wednesday. While a site unpacks a full core update, the new files sit in a predictable folder that's often reachable from the web, installer included. Hit it at the right moment and you can reach code execution. The window lasts seconds, but the security firm Wordfence blocked almost sixty-three thousand attempts in twenty-four hours, with some sources polling the same handful of sites for fourteen and a half hours. Patience is apparently cheap. Update to version seven point one point four, and look for odd requests under the upgrade folder or administrator accounts you don't recognise.

Briefly, from elsewhere. TeamViewer has fixed a flaw that lets the computer you connect to attack yours. A malicious host can crash the viewer, and on Windows, a researcher showed it could go further and run code. Last month's update didn't fix it, so you need version fifteen point eight three. No attacks have been reported.

The hardware wallet maker Trezor is warning buyers from a reseller called CryptoBilis, after a customer in Indonesia reported finding an extra circuit board with a mobile modem inside his wallet, apparently built to send out the recovery phrase. Trezor hasn't examined the device yet, so treat this as a lead only, but it's telling anyone who bought through that reseller since March to move their funds to a new wallet with a new recovery phrase.

The prediction market Polymarket halted trading after a third-party provider was compromised and a script drained about two point one million dollars from users. Hudson Rock names that provider as DebugBear, a website performance service, which has told its customers to remove its embedded code. It's a reminder that every script you load from someone else runs with your site's trust.

Florida's education department says a breach of its student financial aid website exposed data on nearly five million people, including Social Security numbers.

And Kaspersky has found a new loader it calls NetamLoader, built for a customised version of Windows ten used only by government agencies in Asia, and planted in a Windows update folder alongside legitimate signed components. It was found on a single machine and isn't linked to any known group, so details are still emerging.

On to AI security, starting with that extension. For about eleven hours on Thursday, US time, Cursor's extension marketplace served a version of the Claude Code extension that had been tampered with, published under Anthropic's own extension identity. On startup, it added the attacker's key to the user's SSH authorised keys, labelled to look like telemetry, set up persistence, sent details about the machine to the attacker, and tried to open a reverse tunnel. That last step needs administrator rights, which it usually didn't have on Mac and Windows machines. Cursor says at most five thousand users were affected. It pulled the version, shut its marketplace down for a time, rotated its own keys and is contacting the people affected. Anthropic says its own systems weren't compromised. Nobody has yet explained how the package got there. If you installed it, look for an SSH key labelled svc-telemetry, remove the key and the persistence, and rotate your credentials and sessions.

Next, a significant development on ARTEX, the AI penetration testing framework that came up on Wednesday in a South Korean criminal case. CrowdStrike now says a Chinese state-sponsored group it calls Vapor Panda has used it against more than two hundred organisations since the seventeenth of October, including foreign ministries across four regions and US defence, nuclear and semiconductor firms. It confirmed follow-on activity at nine. The framework runs planner and worker agents on top of commercial language models, while a human operator steers. One intrusion used about one point four billion input tokens. The weaknesses the agents exploited were old ones, though: an unsigned login assertion, keys unrotated since twenty twenty-two, and VPN details sitting in someone's sent mail. In CrowdStrike's assessment, the agents let a small team pursue far more targets at once, but most of the gaps they used could have been closed years ago.

The security firm Pluto has found a cryptomining campaign on Hugging Face that came back within days of being taken down. Thirty-one repositories pose as small Qwen test models. Load one with the option that lets a model run its own code, and it installs a miner, and sometimes a reverse shell, on your GPU server. Don't enable that option for models you haven't vetted.

And the start-up Forever Security reports flaws in the built-in browsers of five AI developer tools. In Cursor's case, a web page could pass hidden instructions to the coding agent, which is a prompt injection route to running commands. Cursor fixed that in early October without an advisory. The researchers say flaws in OpenAI's Codex and Microsoft's VS Code are still unpatched. These are the researchers' own claims, and the vendors haven't said much yet.

One industry item, from across the Tasman. Two New Zealand researchers deliberately infected their own machines to plant decoy session cookies in criminals' infostealer logs, then watched for anyone using them. They saw roughly three days between infection and the logs being checked and sold, and logins spiked once they hit the market. For defenders, that's a useful window to revoke sessions after a known infection.

Your priorities for the next forty-eight hours. Critical: Ivanti Connect Secure, upgrade and check for compromise. Critical too: Drupal Commerce Core, and anyone who took the bad Claude Code extension from Cursor's marketplace. High: WordPress seven point one point four and TeamViewer fifteen point eight three. And if you haven't yet, the Cisco firewall and GitLab fixes that US agencies were due to apply by tomorrow.

Looking ahead, watch for Binance's root cause statement and the reopening of withdrawals, more freezes as the stolen funds move, an explanation from Cursor of how the package was published, and a possible CISA listing for the Drupal flaw.
