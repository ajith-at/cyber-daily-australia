Good morning from Melbourne. You're listening to U51, the cyber and AI security brief for Wednesday the twenty-eighth of October, twenty twenty-six, covering the twenty-four hours to six this morning, Melbourne time.

The Australian Signals Directorate has gone on the offensive against an infostealer network and says it cut a criminal off from a hundred and eighty thousand infected computers. The PHP to Shell flaws are now officially on the United States' known exploited list. Microsoft has pulled an Indian certificate authority's root certificates from Windows. And a former penetration tester in South Korea allegedly let an AI framework do much of the work in breaching ninety organisations.

We'll start at home. In an interview with the ABC published on Tuesday, the Australian Signals Directorate, ASD, described one of its offensive cyber operations in unusual detail. The target was Amadey, a piece of malware sold to criminals as a service since twenty eighteen. Each buyer runs their own control server, and infected computers check in with it for orders, usually to install more malware, such as the StealC infostealer, which harvests passwords and browser sessions. ASD says it found Amadey hidden in, among other things, a pirated copy of Photoshop, and saw it on sale on the dark web for about five hundred US dollars.

ASD operators got into the back end of one criminal's Amadey operation and found it controlled about a hundred and eighty thousand computers around the world, including in Australia. The agency's acting deputy director-general of intelligence, Tauri Gregory, said they then disconnected that controller from all of its victims, so the criminal could no longer reach those machines or steal from them. In a separate case, ASD was tipped off that five terabytes of data stolen from a business were up for sale. The business didn't know it had been breached. ASD broke in and destroyed the data first, including an estimated four point eight million files belonging to Australians.

Two points of context. First, this was one operator among many. ESET has tracked more than fifty separate Amadey botnets, so other buyers are unaffected. Second, cutting the connection doesn't undo what was already taken. Infostealer credentials and session cookies can sit unused for months, and a stolen session cookie can get around multi-factor authentication. So if any of your users have ever been caught running cracked software, or you've seen Amadey or StealC detections, treat the passwords and sessions on those machines as burnt and revoke them.

In the same round of interviews, ASD's director-general, Abigail Bradshaw, warned that Australians should expect disruptions to essential services more often as artificial intelligence increases the number and potency of attacks. ASD also says it has set up incident response protocols with the major AI labs, effectively an early warning arrangement, and shared them with all the labs and the Five Eyes partners.

Now to PHP to Shell. On Monday evening, US time, the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency, CISA, added both flaws to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue and gave federal agencies until Thursday to patch. Yesterday we noted they still weren't on the list; that didn't last long. CISA wouldn't describe the exploitation or any victims, and pointed out that it counts attempts as well as successes. Its updated advisory tells organisations to inventory every system running PHP, patch, or take systems offline if they can't be updated straight away, and notes that web application firewalls may buy some time. It links to guidance from partner agencies including Australia's.

The numbers carry a little good news. Censys, which scans the internet, says more than ten million of about forty-two million exposed PHP services have been patched since the twenty-first. That still leaves a very long tail, much of it on versions too old to update at all. The fixed versions remain 8.5.12, 8.4.27, 8.3.36 and 8.2.35, with the dash one builds on Windows.

Staying with patches, Tailscale is racing to fix the Tailworm flaws we covered on Monday, which a research group called Cyberdione Labs published before any fix existed. The report lists eleven findings. The most serious four concern Tailscale SSH, which handles some forwarding with root privileges. An attacker who has already compromised one device can get root on any server that device is allowed to reach by SSH, where policy allows forwarding, and in the lab the chain hopped from machine to machine in under a second each, with no user interaction. Tailscale's engineering lead says the issues mostly concern what happens after a connection is authorised, and that it isn't recommending everyone switch off Tailscale SSH. The company aimed to release a patched client by the end of Tuesday, US time. As of this morning, the latest release on its public code repository is still the affected version, so in the meantime review your SSH access rules and turn off SSH forwarding wherever you don't need it. There are no reports of exploitation.

Next, a trust story. Eight intermediate certificate authority keys belonging to eMudhra, an Indian certificate authority, turned out to be so weak that anyone could recover them by trying just two hundred and fifty-six numbers. Six were used for website certificates and two for code signing, so in the wrong hands they could have produced convincing fake websites or signed software. eMudhra revoked them, and browser makers moved to block them. Microsoft has now gone a step further. On Monday it released out-of-band Windows updates that remove six eMudhra root certificates and a related cross-signed certificate from the Windows trust list, with the same change for its Edge browser scheduled for Tuesday. Most devices get this automatically. The updates matter for machines that are disconnected, can't reach Microsoft's certificate service, or have automatic trust updates switched off. And if any of your own certificates chain back to those roots, they'll need replacing. Microsoft says it's still reviewing eMudhra's other roots.

For network teams, Hewlett Packard Enterprise has patched seventy-two vulnerabilities in the operating systems behind its Aruba wireless gear, thirty-three of them critical and eight at maximum severity, allowing unauthenticated code execution and authentication bypass. Controllers, gateways and access points are all in scope. There's no known exploitation, but that's a lot of critical bugs in one sitting, and edge devices like these have been a favourite target this year. Patch, and keep the management interfaces on their own isolated network segment.

In the United States, the University of New Mexico Health system diverted ambulances for about two hours on Monday after a cyberattack, and suspended outpatient surgeries and appointments for the day. Staff were told to unplug their desktops. Appointments resumed on Tuesday. A new ransomware group calling itself BYOD claims it compromised thirty-six thousand machines and stole data on patients, students and staff, though that claim has not been verified. The system runs the state's only burn unit, children's hospital and top-level trauma centre.

A few briefer items. Apple has backported its fix for an exploited CoreGraphics flaw to older iPhones and iPads on iOS 18, and its latest version 27.2 releases fix hundreds more bugs, including an authorisation issue in Apple Intelligence, which isn't being exploited.

Microsoft has fixed a flaw in Authenticator for Android that let a malicious app with no permissions pretend to be Teams and collect sign-in tokens for a user's work account, enough to read and send their mail. The researchers say no interaction was needed beyond a signed-in account; Microsoft says the user has to complete a sign-in step. Either way, the fix is version 6.2606.4246, and organisations can enforce that minimum through device management.

Kaspersky reports a new Lazarus campaign it calls Operation FlashHole, targeting at least three South Korean organisations with tailored fake résumé and portfolio sites, then abusing mandatory Korean banking security software to load malware. This rests on one vendor's analysis.

And in the United Kingdom, the police inspectorate says suppliers holding police data have been breached repeatedly since twenty twenty-one, and that most supplier contracts include no ongoing security checks at all. If a supplier holds your data, the contract should require ongoing security checks, not just a promise at signing.

On to AI security. South Korean police say a thirty-two-year-old former penetration tester, with six accomplices, breached about ninety organisations, including insurance sellers, golf courses, private academies and hospitals, and stole about eight point two million personal records. He started with conventional scanning tools. Then, in late August, he hooked an AI penetration testing framework called ARTEX up to a coding agent, and, according to police, it picked tools and exploited databases and admin pages largely on its own once given a target. The group sent extortion demands to thirty-eight companies, and was caught early enough that just one small business paid. ARTEX is the same autonomous tool linked to earlier attacks on South Korean banks. This is about the clearest real-world example yet of AI lowering the skill bar for crime.

Also on AI, Warp, a terminal and agentic development tool, says a vendor in its infrastructure was exploited on Friday, exposing agent traces and telemetry for up to about one per cent of its users. Its chief executive says the attack appears to have come from "an agent swarm". The vendor, the flaw, and whether any secrets sitting in those traces were exposed haven't been disclosed, so for now this is the company's account alone. If you use Warp and are notified, follow its instructions, and assume anything that appeared in an agent's output may be in someone else's hands.

That same theme shows up in an infostealer Check Point calls Aokigahara. Alongside the usual browser passwords and crypto wallets, it goes after login and session files for AI tools, including Claude, ChatGPT, Codex, Cursor, Windsurf, GitHub Copilot and Gemini's command line tool, plus Git, SSH and cloud credentials. Dark Reading's headline treats a developer's AI assistants as a keyring. The details come from a single vendor's report, but the direction is clear: AI sessions are now loot.

And in industry news, Anthropic has filed to go public. Of the three hundred and thirty-five pages in its filing, ninety-three are risk factors, including the possibility that autonomous systems take harmful actions at scale without human intervention, and that AI progresses beyond the company's ability to evaluate and safeguard its own models. It's not often a prospectus doubles as a threat model.

Your priorities for the next forty-eight hours. Critical: finish PHP patching, and assess anything left exposed since last Wednesday. Also critical for Aruba shops: the HPE updates, with management interfaces locked down. High: install the Tailscale client update as soon as it lands, and disable SSH forwarding until then; push the eMudhra update to disconnected Windows machines and check your certificate chains; enforce the latest Authenticator on Android; and update Apple devices. And as hygiene, if a developer's machine ever caught an infostealer, rotate their AI tool tokens along with everything else.

Looking ahead, expect Tailscale's advisory and release within a day or two, more reporting on what attackers are doing with PHP to Shell now it's formally on the list, and more detail, and possibly a data leak, from New Mexico.
