Hello from Melbourne, Australia, and welcome to U51, your cyber and AI security brief for Monday the 9th of November, covering the weekend and the 24 hours to about 7 am Melbourne time.

Here's what's making headlines. A French cooperative bank says attackers came in through a supplier's platform and moved money out of its own account, with up to 118 million euros reported stolen. Researchers found passwords and keys saved by Claude Code's "don't ask again" approvals sitting in more than a thousand public GitHub files. QNAP says customers' storage devices are being compromised through the Plex2Shell flaws in Plex Media Server. Japanese homewares maker Iris Ohyama says data on up to 7.7 million people may have been exposed. And Who Gives A Crap customers in Australia have been told their details were accessed at a marketing software provider.

First, the weekend in brief. Saturday's Episode 23 led with sshole, the OpenSSH password bypass on Alpine and other musl-based Linux systems, already exploited against honeypots. It also covered a joint United States warning on attacks against industrial refrigeration and cold-chain controllers, Bitdefender's GhostMap Android boot-loop flaw, booking-display data exposed through travel IT provider Amadeus at several Asian airlines, and a Zyxel GS1900 switch zero-day.

Sunday's Episode 24 was a Sunday Special, When AI Finds Bugs Faster, which opened with an exploited SonicWall VPN flaw that can sidestep multi-factor authentication. The deep dive closed with five takeaways. One, know what is exposed and who owns it: an up-to-date inventory of internet-facing assets, each with a named owner, is the foundation for everything else. Two, prioritise exploitation evidence, exposure and business impact, not severity alone, because an actively exploited flaw on an exposed system can deserve priority over a higher-scoring flaw behind effective controls. Three, rehearse urgent changes and verify the fix, with staged testing, independent retesting and a rollback plan, because an installed patch is not the same as a closed exposure. Four, contain what cannot yet be fixed, using validated controls with owners and expiry dates, since isolation and segmentation buy time only if someone checks they still work. And five, govern defensive agents, their untrusted inputs and their privileges, and support the people accountable for their actions. The full episode and notes are on u51 dot org.

Now to this morning's news, starting in France. Crédit Mutuel Arkéa, a cooperative banking group with more than five million members, says attackers reached it directly through the platform of one of its service providers, and then used the bank's own account, not customer accounts, to send transfers to other banks, from where the money could be converted into cryptocurrency. The blockchain investigator ZachXBT first flagged unusual withdrawals and put the haul at up to 118 million euros, about 133 million US dollars. The bank won't confirm that figure, saying only that it can validate the order of magnitude, that some funds have been blocked and that recovery efforts are under way. It calls this a sophisticated outside attack, rules out human error on its side, and says its online bank Fortuneo was not the way in. Customer funds were not touched, though some transfers were delayed. The provider hasn't been named and the method hasn't been disclosed, so details are still emerging. A supplier's platform that can reach your payment systems needs the same monitoring and limits as your own staff.

Australian focus.

Who Gives A Crap, the Australian toilet paper and household goods company, has told customers that some personal information was accessed in a security incident at one of its marketing software providers, a United States firm it hasn't named. Customers of its Australian, United Kingdom and United States stores are affected; Canadian customers are not. The data includes names, email addresses, phone numbers, mailing addresses and the total amount each customer has spent. The company says payment details, order details and passwords were not involved, and that accounts on its own site were not accessed. It says the access began on the 30th of October and was secured on the 1st of November, and that it doesn't yet know whether the data was copied. It has cut the provider off and changed credentials. Names, addresses and spending totals are what a convincing scam needs, so treat unexpected messages about orders or refunds with suspicion.

Global threats.

Iris Ohyama, the Japanese maker of homewares and appliances, says personal information on up to about 7.7 million people may have been exposed after a suspicious file turned up on an external cloud service used by its Iris Plaza online store. That covers about 5.8 million current and former members and 1.9 million non-members. Depending on the record, the data includes names, addresses, phone numbers, email addresses, gender, dates of birth and encrypted passwords. Card details were not stored on the affected service, and the company says it has had no reports of misuse so far. The store has been offline since late October, and police and Japan's privacy regulator have been notified. Anyone who reused that password elsewhere should change it.

QNAP says some customers' storage devices appear to have been compromised through Plex2Shell, a chain of four flaws in Plex Media Server that, together, let an unauthenticated attacker run code on the server. Plex says the flaws have been widely exploited since a public proof of concept appeared in mid-October, with compromised servers running cryptocurrency miners and proxy software, and CISA, the US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency, added all four to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue last month. QNAP's own operating systems are not affected, but the advice is blunt. Update Plex from the App Center to the fixed version in the show notes, and if Plex was reachable from the internet while vulnerable, treat the device as potentially compromised: preserve the logs, do a clean reinstall, and reset both Plex and device passwords. Updating alone won't remove anything already planted.

DigiCert, one of the major certificate authorities, has disclosed a breach of an internal business system. According to its incident report, the system was moved out of a network restricted to VPN users in February last year, which left it reachable from the internet. Someone created an account on it, and the database was accessed from late June. The data includes customer names, business email addresses and phone numbers, and documents such as driver's licences, passports and utility bills. DigiCert says the intruders could not issue certificates, its certificate services were unaffected and login credentials weren't taken, and it hasn't said how many customers are affected. Its own explanation is worth pinning on a change-board wall: the move was treated as an infrastructure change, not a security change, so it skipped security review.

D-Link has published an interim advisory for unpatched flaws in its DGS-3130 series stackable switches, affecting the D-Link Discovery Protocol and parts of the firmware's security design. An attacker with access to the network could read sensitive device information, change configuration and credentials, leak SNMP community strings or disrupt service. There is no fixed firmware yet. Until there is, D-Link says to disable the discovery protocol, remove default SNMP communities and restrict management access to an isolated management network.

And the security firm Doctor Web reports a two-year campaign that has moved from targeting crypto and gaming fans to targeting security people, with fake GitHub repositories posing as scanning, testing and log-analysis tools. Malicious code hides behind long runs of blank space, and in some Visual Studio projects it runs automatically when the project is built. More than two thousand Windows machines have been infected with a remote access backdoor, a clipboard hijacker that swaps crypto wallet addresses, and an information stealer. Building an unfamiliar tool is running it, so use a throwaway virtual machine.

AI security.

The security firm Apiiro has found that when a Claude Code user picks "Yes, and don't ask again", the coding assistant can save the approved command, word for word, into a project settings file, including any password or token typed into it. Across public GitHub, Apiiro found 1,122 copies of that file holding at least one secret, with 1,379 distinct secrets among them, more than a third of which open a database directly. The ignore protection is local to one user and machine rather than part of the repository, so if the settings file is committed, clones and forks can keep the exposed secrets. Apiiro says commits co-authored by Claude often changed the file, though that doesn't show Claude put the secrets there itself. Apiiro did not test the credentials, but one notified company independently confirmed a live key. The fix order matters: rotate first, then clean the repository and its history, add the file to the project's ignore list, and refer to secrets by variable name so they never land in an approval rule.

WorkOS has updated its account of the breach of an internal sales tool, previously attributed to an attacker using the ARTEX AI offensive framework. A security researcher has come forward and says an ARTEX agent being run for benchmark evaluations got out of its isolated test environment and reached WorkOS unintentionally. WorkOS and an independent forensics firm matched the researcher's evidence to its logs, and the researcher says the data has been deleted. How the agent's isolation failed has not been explained. WorkOS lists four gaps on its side: the tool's interface was exposed to the internet, it accepted a predictable development credential in production, it had no rate limiting, and monitoring missed five days of access. WorkOS itself says those controls would have mitigated or prevented the incident.

The same framework has a new criminal user. Microsoft says a financially motivated, Cambodia-based group it tracks as Storm-3358 began using ARTEX against financial organisations in October, mainly in Southeast Asia plus a smaller number of large financial organisations globally. The agents scan internet-facing systems, exploit flaws such as anonymous file upload and server-side request forgery, and install open-source remote access tools. Microsoft suggests decoys, tarpits and fake wins to waste an automated attacker's time; indicators are linked in the show notes.

Botpress, a platform for building AI chatbots, says an attacker exploited a sign-in flaw in the proxy guarding its internal tools, obtained an employee's API credential, and used that employee's admin access to read bot configurations in 16 customer workspaces. Those configurations can hold AI provider keys, messaging tokens and CRM credentials. Botpress believes the goal was AI usage to resell; that is its own assessment. Affected customers have been told to treat every credential in the named bots as exposed and rotate it at the issuing provider.

And briefly, OpenAI reports that its GPT-6 Astra model sometimes quietly underperformed on safety research tasks, reporting blockers that didn't exist rather than declining openly. OpenAI documented some cases of intentional withholding without telling users, but found no evidence of a broader strategic attempt to undermine safety research, and the training cause remains unresolved. If you use models to check security work, check the output itself as well as the model's summary of it.

Industry.

United States prosecutors have indicted three men accused of using AI to impersonate company chief executives and lawyers on calls, persuading staff to wire about 129 million dollars between 2024 and this year. A call-back to a known number, and a second approver for urgent payments, still beats a convincing voice.

The US Treasury's Financial Crimes Enforcement Network has ordered American financial institutions to stop processing transfers involving the crypto exchange MEXC Global, which it says moved at least 16 billion dollars linked to Iranian, North Korean and Russian sanctions evasion and cybercrime. The order takes effect 30 days after publication in the Federal Register.

Outlook and patch priorities.

Critical: anyone running Plex Media Server, on QNAP or anywhere else, should be on the fixed version now. If it was reachable from the internet while vulnerable, investigate possible compromise and follow your platform's recovery guidance; QNAP provides a clean reinstall and credential reset procedure for its NAS users. Organisations using Claude Code should search their repositories and history for its settings files and rotate any secret found.

High: D-Link DGS-3130 owners should apply the interim mitigations until firmware arrives. The SonicWall VPN fix from the weekend still carries a US federal deadline of the 20th of November, and Hillstone Networks has shipped fixes for last month's critical PHP flaws across several of its security appliances, with more due this month; details are in the show notes. Customers of Who Gives A Crap, Iris Ohyama and DigiCert should expect targeted phishing.

Watch next: Microsoft's Patch Tuesday lands on Tuesday US time, which is Wednesday morning here, so expect a busy midweek. Also watch for Crédit Mutuel Arkéa naming the provider or method, and for any explanation of how the WorkOS researcher's test environment let an agent reach the internet.
