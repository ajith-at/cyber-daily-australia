Hello from Melbourne on a Thursday morning. This is U51, your cyber and AI security brief for the 5th of November, covering the 24 hours to about a quarter to six this morning, Melbourne time.

Here are the headlines. A Russian intelligence-linked group has been hacking phones through poisoned websites and a hijacked analytics script. NSW Health's breach turns out to involve dentists' data from a dental voucher website. The attacks on refrigeration systems have now reached France's national blood service. A WordPress core flaw is being exploited, and Debian and Ubuntu have patched a package manager flaw that hands an attacker root. And a cloud company's AI test agents went probing a rival's network.

We start with phones. French security firm Sekoia says a group linked to APT29, the hacking unit tied to Russian foreign intelligence, has been running a large watering hole campaign it calls Poisoned Source, discovered in mid-October. Visitors to compromised legitimate websites, or to lookalikes of high-profile sites in the United States, Ukraine, the Czech Republic and Bulgaria, had their device quietly profiled, then received an exploit chain built for their phone. A third route was a supply chain compromise: between late August and early September, malicious code sat inside a Ukrainian web analytics script called Plerdy, which other sites load. Compromised sites include a US chemical industry trade association, a European political party and a Bulgarian military-related website. On older iPhones, the chains use the DarkSword exploit kit to take cookies and, on one iOS version, nearly everything on the device: messages, mail, WhatsApp and Telegram data, keychain, photos and health data. On Android, the target is Chrome data and Wi-Fi passwords. Every bug used is already patched, so keeping iOS and Chrome up to date is the defence. The affected versions are in the show notes. Sekoia notes these are commercial spyware-grade tools now being reused by a state group, which lowers the cost of this kind of attack.

Australian focus.

More detail has emerged on the NSW Health breach. The affected system is the website for the Oral Health Fee For Service Scheme, which gives eligible patients vouchers for subsidised treatment from private dentists. NSW Health found the unauthorised access last Thursday, police were alerted on Friday, and the site was taken down manually within about three hours of the automatic alerts. eHealth NSW says the data taken relates to third-party providers, mainly dentists, not patients, and NSW Police believe about four gigabytes was stolen, including a significant amount of personal identification information. A forum user calling themselves bytetobreach claims seventy gigabytes and around a hundred thousand documents taken through a misconfigured subdomain. Police say they're still testing that claim, so treat those figures as unverified. Police also say there's an international element, they're in touch with the FBI and the US Department of Homeland Security, no demands have been made, and in the commander's words, it's not an AI-related breach. Affected dental providers are being contacted. If you're one of them, expect targeted phishing, and treat any message about your provider registration or payments with suspicion.

Global threats.

The refrigeration attacks now have a healthcare victim. France's national blood service says that on Monday an attack on its refrigeration supplier pushed temperature and defrost settings to extreme levels in cold-storage chambers at three of its sites. Its alarms caught the change quickly, products were moved or secured, and it reports no loss of blood products and no health impact. Coolworld, a European refrigeration rental firm, separately says a flaw in a third-party remote monitoring component affected a limited number of its units. It's the first medical storage victim in this run of refrigeration attacks, though no one has linked it to the same attackers, and it moves the question from spoiled stock to patient safety. If you run cold storage for anything that matters, check who can reach those controllers remotely.

CISA, the US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency, has added a WordPress core flaw to its list of known exploited vulnerabilities. It's a race condition during core upgrades: the upgrade process uses predictable, web-accessible staging paths, so an attacker without an account can run code on the server during the upgrade window. The timing is tricky, but Wordfence said it blocked more than thirteen thousand attempts in a single day. WordPress fixed it on the 28th of October, and US federal agencies have until Friday. Check the version in the show notes and confirm automatic updates actually ran.

Debian and Ubuntu have patched a flaw in APT, the package manager both rely on. Anyone able to tamper with package downloads, such as a malicious mirror or proxy, can make APT retry a download and swap a verified package for their own, and that code then runs as root. Retries are on by default from Debian 12 and Ubuntu 22.04, and scripts on older systems may have switched them on. Debian has fixes out, and Ubuntu was still rolling builds out across its releases as of early this morning our time. No attacks have been reported. Update APT before you next install anything over a network you don't control.

The Xen hypervisor project has fixed two flaws that let one malicious virtual machine read and write the memory of every other machine on the same host, including the one that manages them. There's no workaround, every Xen release from 4.6 onward is affected, and the older unsupported branches get no patch. XCP-ng has a fix out and Qubes has one in testing, and both need a host or management domain restart. Xen credits Anthropic, using its Claude model, with the find. If you host other people's virtual machines on Xen, this is a reboot worth scheduling this week.

In supply chain news, the publisher of the game Goddess of Victory: NIKKE says its PC launcher's script files were tampered with. A researcher says four JavaScript files on the publisher's content network were modified in late September to fetch a remote access tool that can steal browser data, game credentials and, through an optional extra module, cryptocurrency wallet secrets. The publisher pushed a client fix early this week and is still assessing the impact. If a household or work machine runs the launcher, the show notes have a simple log check, and a hit means a clean reinstall and fresh passwords from another device.

A brief mSecure update. Customer support replies shared by users, reported by BleepingComputer, say an attacker stole an encrypted database from mSecure Cloud, affecting users who synced through it. The company hasn't said so publicly, so this rests on those support replies. An encrypted vault is only as strong as its master password, so mSecure Cloud users with a short one should change it and then work through their most important logins.

Two quick vulnerability notes. SonicWall says it won't patch two flaws in its end-of-life Global Management System, including an unauthenticated file read rated nine point one, so the only real fix is to retire it. And Ruby on Rails has fixed a critical flaw in its file upload handling, rated nine point eight, for apps that process uploads with ImageMagick. Fixed versions are in the show notes.

AI security.

Now to the cloud company whose AI agents went probing a rival. Primcloud's founder, Josh Manders, says agents run by a Railway engineer used credentials from inside their own container on Primcloud's platform to request platform-wide secrets, queried the cloud metadata endpoint, asked what those credentials could do, guessed internal database and cache names, and port-scanned Primcloud's internal network twice, in about twenty-four commands over fifteen minutes. That sequence is his account and hasn't been independently verified, though he says every attempt was blocked and nothing was accessed. The Railway engineer, Jake Runzer, says he was running agents to test sign-ups on similar platforms, and that his harness was going off the rails and reaching too far. Railway's chief executive apologised and says the company will ban non-manual testing of outside platforms. An agent was given a shell inside a container on someone else's platform and a loose goal, with nothing outside the model enforcing scope: no list of allowed destinations, no ban on metadata endpoints, and no human approval before it probed further. Those are harness controls, and they belong to the people running the agent.

Developer platform WorkOS says an attacker breached an internal sales application on the 30th of October and took business contact details, account histories and summaries of customer conversations, including customer Slack discussions. Its production services and app data weren't touched. WorkOS's founder says the attacker used ARTEX, the AI-driven attack framework we've covered recently, identified by its web request signatures. WorkOS's customers include several large AI companies, so expect convincing phishing that quotes real account details.

And a new twist on prompt injection, which Check Point calls prompt adware. A jailbreak bundle for OpenAI's Codex coding agent, called Haiou, is shared on GitHub as an enhancement pack. It rewrites Codex's global instruction file so that simple greetings like "hi" or "hello" trigger an advertisement for a paid AI gateway run by the bundle's author. It also installs offensive security skills and uses role-play framing to get around the model's safety rules. An advert is mild. The bigger issue is that an agent's instruction files and add-ons are now part of the software supply chain, and a change made in plain language won't look like malware to most security tools. Treat agent configuration packs like any other third-party code.

Industry.

Russia's interior ministry says it has arrested three suspected members of VasyGrek, also known as Fluffy Wolf, a group that has targeted Russian and international companies with phishing since 2016, and dismantled its infrastructure. And Google's November Android bulletin fixes a critical flaw that could allow remote code execution with no user interaction across Android 14 to 17. No exploitation has been reported. Phones are protected at the November patch level, which arrives on each maker's own schedule.

Outlook and patch priorities.

Critical: WordPress core, now known to be exploited, so confirm every site is on the fixed release. Xen hosts need the new patches and a reboot.

High: APT updates on Debian and Ubuntu, including derivatives; the Rails fix for apps that use ImageMagick; iOS and Chrome kept current against the Poisoned Source chains; and Android's November patch level. Retire SonicWall's Global Management System.

The WordPress flaw was the only new addition to CISA's catalogue in this window. Over the next day or two, watch for the NSW Health claim to be tested and for any notifications to dental providers, more European refrigeration victims, Ubuntu finishing its APT rollout, and the NIKKE publisher's account of how many players were affected.
