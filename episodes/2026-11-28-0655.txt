Hello from Melbourne, this is U51, your cyber and AI security brief for Saturday the 28th of November, covering the 24 hours to about six this morning, Melbourne time.

Here's what's making headlines. The US Air Force's main cloud platform has reportedly been breached through an unidentified software flaw. SAP has confirmed a security incident, with insiders saying stolen staff tokens were used to copy and change source code on GitHub. A researcher has published a working exploit for an unpatched flaw in the Cacti network monitoring tool. And Britain's welfare department and the French news agency AFP have both pulled online services after cyber incidents.

Our first story is the US Air Force. Reuters reports that hackers breached Cloud One, the Air Force's enterprise cloud platform, which has been running since 2017 and hosts Defense Department applications for military operations, personnel, logistics and maintenance. According to Reuters, the intrusion was found within the past month during an upgrade of the platform, and it disrupted application migrations and software updates. One source told Reuters the attackers got in through an unidentified software vulnerability, potentially exposing sensitive operational and logistics information. Who is behind it, how far they got and whether any data was taken are all unknown, and US Cyber Command has reportedly been brought in to assess the damage. The Air Force declined to comment on the status of its systems. This rests on anonymous sourcing, so treat the detail with some caution. It also lands in an uncomfortable week for Washington, following reported breaches of the FBI's National Crime Information Center, a CIA open-source analysis system and the Treasury's Financial Crimes Enforcement Network. Nothing public links these incidents, and it would be a mistake to assume a single campaign. The lesson for defenders is a familiar one: this was discovered during an upgrade rather than by monitoring, which is a reminder that platform changes are often when long-standing intrusions finally surface.

Global threats.

SAP, one of the world's largest enterprise software vendors, has confirmed a security incident. The company told BleepingComputer it identified suspicious activity, took measures to protect customers, and has found no evidence so far of unauthorised access to customer business data. The more worrying detail comes from elsewhere. German magazine Der Spiegel, citing an internal memo, says SAP is tracking the incident as Project Granite, and insiders say the attackers stole employee authentication tokens and used them to copy and modify SAP source code on GitHub, possibly with help from AI tools. SAP hasn't confirmed those details, named the repositories involved, or said whether any modified code went anywhere near a customer. Coming days after 1Password described a breach that also began with a hijacked GitHub account, the pattern is hard to miss: developer accounts and tokens are now a front door to the software supply chain. SAP customers should watch for advisories rather than act on rumour, but this is a good weekend to review who holds long-lived tokens in your own source code platforms.

Next, an unpatched hole in Cacti, the open-source tool many organisations use to monitor and graph their routers, switches, firewalls and servers. A researcher has published a technical write-up, a proof-of-concept exploit and a video showing how an attacker with no login can get command execution in just four web requests. The flaw is a stored SQL injection in the graph display settings of the guest account. The attacker saves malicious database commands in those preferences, and when Cacti processes them, the commands change the configuration of its polling engine so it runs the attacker's operating system commands. The guest account has to be enabled for this to work, and it has been switched off by default for many years. The researcher demonstrated root access, though watchTowr notes the privileges depend on the account Cacti runs as, which is often a low-privileged web server account. The tested version is in the show notes, and it's unclear whether older releases are affected. No fix had been published at the time of writing. The reason this matters is that Cacti servers often hold device credentials and network management secrets, so a compromised monitoring box can become a map and a key ring for the rest of the network. Disable guest access and keep the web interface off the internet.

In Britain, the Department for Work and Pensions, the government's largest department by spending, has taken some online services offline as a precaution after a cyber incident. The department says regular benefit and pension payments are not affected and will be paid as normal, and that it is working with the National Cyber Security Centre and the Government Cyber Coordination Centre to restore services. It hasn't said what kind of incident it is dealing with, so details are still emerging.

In France, the news agency Agence France-Presse says it was hit by a cyberattack overnight on Thursday, Paris time. The target was AFP Forum, the platform media clients use to browse and download the agency's stories, photos and video. AFP took the platform offline as soon as the intrusion was detected, and it remains unavailable until further notice, with subscribers offered other ways to get content. AFP hasn't said who was behind it, whether any data was accessed, or whether other systems were affected. Australian newsrooms that rely on AFP feeds may notice the workaround arrangements.

Two supply-chain malware stories show how attackers keep borrowing trusted distribution channels. Security firm eSentire describes an attack that starts on a fake site for a screenshot tool, which serves a Microsoft-signed installer. That installs an app called Setup Assistant, published on the Microsoft Store, which acts as a backdoor, runs commands supplied by the attacker and then deploys a previously undocumented remote access trojan called Vinci RAT. The trojan switches off a built-in Windows malware-scanning interface and checks in every ten minutes through a scheduled task. Separately, South Korean firm AhnLab found that installers for two free Windows utilities, the Q-Dir file manager and DesktopOK, were tampered with on the developer's official site, and even through the programs' update process. The poisoned installers run the real program while quietly installing a Python-based remote access tool. How the files were replaced isn't known. The affected versions and the indicators for both campaigns are in the show notes.

In Southeast Asia, threat intelligence firm SOCRadar has detailed a suspected Vietnam-linked espionage group it calls WeaveLotus. SOCRadar says the group carried out five intrusions in Thailand and the Philippines and reconnoitred Indonesian government organisations between August and September, hunting for intelligence on South China Sea defence agreements. Initial access came mostly through web application flaws and default credentials in open-source software such as GeoServer, JasperReports and osTicket, along with exposed Laravel debug pages. The Vietnam link is the vendor's assessment, based on language in the code, working hours and targeting, so treat the attribution as a lead rather than settled.

Japan's education ministry has disclosed that hackers breached its government procurement information system, an intrusion it detected back on the 1st of October. About 164,000 records containing company names, representatives' names and other business details, plus about 1,700 email addresses, may have been exposed. The ministry has cut the system off from other networks and says it has seen no sign the data has been published or misused.

A quick follow-up on stories from earlier this week. The US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency, or CISA, has added the two exploited GeoServer flaws and the latest exploited Drupal core flaw to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue, giving US federal agencies until the 30th of November, which is Monday in the US and Tuesday here, to fix them. If you run either and haven't patched, the clock has formally started.

AI security.

The only new AI angle in the window is the insider claim that SAP's attackers may have used AI tools. That is unconfirmed, and nothing has been said about how such tools would have been used, so it's a detail to watch rather than a finding.

Industry.

Microsoft has confirmed that Windows Server 2028 will drop the Windows Server Update Services role entirely. The tool, long used by IT teams to control which updates reach their fleets, was deprecated in 2024. It stays supported in earlier versions, including Windows Server 2025, for their full lifecycles, and those servers can still deliver updates to the new release. Organisations running disconnected or tightly controlled networks now have a firm reason to plan their next update model.

Microsoft has also pushed an emergency out-of-band hotpatch for Windows Server 2025, fixing crashes that followed this month's hotpatch on some servers using time-limited IPv6 routes. It's a non-security fix, it installs automatically and it doesn't need a restart, which, for once, makes it the most convenient patch of the month.

Outlook and patch priorities.

First, Cacti: disable the guest account, restrict the web interface to trusted networks, review the polling configuration for anything unexpected, and watch for a vendor fix. Second, GeoServer and Drupal, now on CISA's exploited list. Third, check Windows endpoints for the Setup Assistant app and for the tampered Q-Dir and DesktopOK versions listed in the show notes, and reinstall clean copies. Over the next day or two, watch for SAP to say which code was affected, for any confirmed exploitation of the Cacti flaw, and for more detail from the Department for Work and Pensions and the US Air Force.

That's U51 for this Saturday. Enjoy the weekend, and keep those guest accounts switched off.
